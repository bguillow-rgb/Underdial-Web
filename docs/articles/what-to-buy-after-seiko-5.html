What to Buy After a Seiko 5: Your Upgrade Guide for 2026

By Underdial · Published November 13, 2026

Quick Answer

After a Seiko 5, the most logical upgrades are the Seiko Prospex Turtle ($280, $350, adds 200m ISO dive rating), Seiko Presage Cocktail Time ($350, $500, better finishing and movement), or a Swiss automatic like the Hamilton Khaki Field or Certina DS Action Diver, all under $1,000 and all meaningfully better.

The Seiko 5 is probably the most common first automatic watch on the planet, and for good reason. It’s honest, affordable, and costs less than a dinner out for two. But once you’ve worn it for a year, you start noticing the Hardlex crystal, the wobbly bracelet clasp, and the fact that every other person at the watch meetup is also wearing one. That itch for something more is completely normal. The question is: what actually counts as a meaningful upgrade, and where do you spend your money?

”I love my Seiko 5 — why would I upgrade at all?”

A question we hear often: and it’s a fair one. The Seiko 5 (SRPD family) runs the 4R36 movement, hacks, hand-winds, and keeps time within roughly ±15 seconds per day. That’s genuinely usable. The case finishing is decent for the money. So what’s missing?

A few things tend to bother Seiko 5 owners over time: the mineral Hardlex crystal scratches more easily than sapphire, the bracelet end links flex and the clasp can feel loose, and ±15 s/day accuracy is fine but not impressive. None of these are dealbreakers. But when you move to the next tier, even by $150, you can fix all three at once.

The other reason to upgrade is style gap coverage. Under $200, the Seiko 5 Sports SRPD55 is essentially unbeatable as an automatic. From $200, $350, the Seiko Prospex Turtle adds genuine dive-watch character and a 200m rating. And from $350, $500, the Seiko Presage Cocktail Time is widely considered the most beautiful watch Seiko makes at that price. These aren’t lateral moves — they’re a coherent upgrade ladder.

”What’s the most logical first step up from a Seiko 5 — staying within Seiko?”

This one comes up a lot: and the answer splits cleanly by what you want from the upgrade.

If you want a better tool watch: the Seiko Prospex line is the obvious next move. The Seiko 5 Sports SRPD family runs $150, $230 at authorized dealers; the Prospex SRPE line sits at $230, $350. That price gap buys real-world improvements: sapphire crystal instead of mineral, a ceramic bezel insert instead of aluminum, and a 200m water resistance rating versus the SRPD’s 100m. That last point matters if you swim or snorkel, since the Prospex qualifies as a true ISO 6425 dive watch and the Seiko 5 does not.

If you want better finishing and a more refined everyday piece: skip to the Seiko Presage. The 6R35 movement inside the Presage runs at ±10 seconds per day with a 70-hour power reserve, a genuine step above the 4R36 in both accuracy and convenience. The sunburst dials and applied indices are in another league visually. The existing Underdial guide on the Seiko Presage covers this in depth, but the short version is: if you want one watch that works with a sport coat, the Presage beats the Prospex.

”What about Orient — is that a real step up or just a sideways move?”

Sideways in price, but with meaningful differences in finishing. The Orient Star, Orient’s premium tier at typically $400, $650, uses a proprietary in-house movement with an open heart display, power reserve indicator, and significantly better case and dial finishing than either the Seiko 5 or standard Orient.

If you’re currently wearing a Seiko 5 dress-dial variant and want to level up without crossing into Swiss territory, the Orient Star is worth serious consideration. The tradeoff: Orient Star’s movement accuracy (around ±10 s/day) is comparable to the Presage’s 6R35, but the brand is less recognized outside enthusiast circles. That matters to exactly zero serious watch people, but it’s worth knowing before you buy. The Seiko Presage vs Orient Star comparison breaks down exactly where each earns its price.

”Should my next watch after a Seiko 5 be Swiss?”

Readers frequently ask: this, and the honest answer is: not necessarily, but crossing into Swiss territory does change what you’re getting in a few specific ways.

The sub-$1,000 segment in 2026 offers Swiss automatic movements from Tissot, Hamilton, and Certina alongside Japanese in-house calibers from Seiko and Orient Star, with sapphire crystals and build quality that rivals watches at two to three times the price. So “Swiss” isn’t a magic word. That said, two Swiss watches consistently make sense as Seiko 5 upgrades.

Hamilton Khaki Field Automatic (~$495, $750): This is the watch the watch community most consistently recommends as the ideal first Swiss automatic for someone coming from the Seiko 5 tier. You get an 80-hour power reserve (nearly double the Seiko 5’s roughly 41 hours), 100m water resistance, and genuine military heritage in a 38mm case that wears well on a wide range of wrists. The finishing isn’t flashy, but it’s purposeful.

Certina DS Action Diver (~$775, $950): This is the upgrade if you want a Swiss dive watch specifically. It’s ISO 6425-certified to 300m, carries a Powermatic 80 movement, ceramic bezel, sapphire crystal, and comes in with a compelling gilt dial. Compared to the Seiko Prospex Turtle, you’re paying roughly $500 more, but you’re getting Swiss manufacture, a ceramic bezel, and an 80-hour power reserve in return. That’s not nothing.

”Is the Citizen Promaster Diver worth considering as a Seiko 5 upgrade?”

A question we hear often: especially from buyers who are tired of remembering to wind or wear their watch. The Citizen Promaster Diver is a compelling detour from the automatic path. You trade the mechanical rotor for Eco-Drive solar, which means you will almost certainly never interact with a crown again in normal use. It’s lightweight and slim for a 44mm case, always accurate, and requires no battery changes.

The honest catch: it’s a quartz watch, and some people find that emotionally unsatisfying after falling for automatics. If the mechanical nature of the Seiko 5 is part of why you wear it, the Citizen Promaster will solve your dive-spec problems while removing the thing you probably love. For buyers who are genuinely pragmatic about movements, though, it’s hard to beat on value and zero-maintenance convenience. The Seiko Prospex vs Citizen Promaster guide digs into exactly this tradeoff.

”How do I know if I’m actually ready to spend more, or just bored of my current watch?”

This is the most underrated question in the whole conversation. Watch upgrade FOMO is real, and buying a $700 watch when you’re simply bored of your SRPD dial is a mistake you’ll recognize at 2am when you’re browsing again six months later.

A quick self-audit before you spend: Have you worn the Seiko 5 at least 100 times? Have you swapped its strap (a $20 leather or NATO completely transforms the feel)? Do you find yourself reaching past it for another watch, or still reaching for it? If you’re still wearing it regularly, the upgrade is optional. If it’s sitting in the drawer, you’ve genuinely outgrown it and a step up will stick.

5 FAQs on upgrading from a Seiko 5

Is the Seiko 5 worth keeping after I upgrade? Yes. The Seiko 5 makes a great rotation beater even after you’ve moved up. Its low stakes mean you wear it hard — to the beach, the gym, rainy commutes — without anxiety. Many collectors keep theirs for years as the go-anywhere piece.

What’s the best upgrade from a Seiko 5 under $500? The Seiko Presage Cocktail Time is the strongest under-$500 upgrade. You get a better-regulated 6R35 movement (±10 s/day, 70-hour reserve), sunburst dial finishing that rivals watches costing twice as much, and noticeably improved case and bracelet quality.

What’s the best Swiss upgrade from a Seiko 5 under $1,000? The Hamilton Khaki Field Automatic ($495, $750) and the Certina DS Action Diver ($775, $950) are the two strongest Swiss moves. Hamilton gives you military heritage and an 80-hour reserve; Certina gives you an ISO 6425-certified 300m diver with ceramic bezel and sapphire crystal.

How much should I spend on my second watch after a Seiko 5? The $300, $600 band offers the best value jump. At that range you gain sapphire crystal, a better-regulated movement, and improved bracelet quality — upgrades you notice every day. Beyond $600 the gains are real but more incremental.

Should my next watch after a Seiko 5 be in a different style? Ideally, yes. If your Seiko 5 is a sports/casual piece, a dress-leaning upgrade (Seiko Presage, Orient Star) adds versatility. If your Seiko 5 is a dressy dial variant, a true tool watch (Prospex, Citizen Promaster, Hamilton field) rounds out the box.
