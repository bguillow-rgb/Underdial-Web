Frederique Constant vs Tissot: Which Should You Buy?

By Underdial · Published October 9, 2026

Quick Answer

Tissot is the better buy for most people under $700, broader range, proven sporty and dress models, and excellent after-sales support. Frederique Constant makes sense if you want a refined dress watch and are open to buying pre-owned, where Classics models regularly appear well under $800.

Both brands carry a “Swiss Made” stamp. Both target the same crowded slice of the market sitting just below the traditional luxury threshold. But buying one over the other is a genuinely different decision, different movement philosophy, different aesthetics, different ownership experience. Here is the honest comparison.

So what actually separates these two brands?

Tissot, founded in 1853, sits inside the Swatch Group alongside Longines, Omega, and a dozen others. That ownership means deep manufacturing infrastructure, a global service network, and the ability to produce large volumes of well-finished watches at competitive prices. The PRX, Le Locle, Gentleman, and Seastar lines each address a different buyer with real conviction.

Frederique Constant is newer by comparison: the brand was founded in 1988, which in watchmaking terms makes it practically a startup. But it built credibility quickly by investing in its own movement production. The brand has now released 35 manufacture movements, an unusual commitment for a brand operating at accessible price points. Its Classics and Slimline collections lean heavily on traditional dress-watch aesthetics: clean dials, Roman numerals, guilloche textures, slim cases.

The short version: Tissot is a versatile all-rounder; Frederique Constant is a dress-watch specialist with higher ambitions that sometimes outpace its budget.

What does each brand actually cost — and is the comparison even fair?

This one comes up a lot: buyers assume the two brands compete dollar-for-dollar. They don’t, quite.

A Tissot PRX Powermatic 80 retails for around $625, with a stainless steel case, integrated bracelet, 100m water resistance, and an 80-hour movement. The Le Locle Powermatic 80 comes in a little lower. The Gentleman sits around $495-$595 depending on configuration. All are new-watch prices at authorised dealers.

Frederique Constant’s Classics collection features 40mm cases with sapphire crystals, but new retail pricing starts around $1,295 and climbs toward $1,995 for complication models. That puts most of the new lineup above the $1,000 ceiling that most buyers in this category are working with.

Here is where the pre-owned market changes the conversation entirely. Pre-owned Frederique Constant Classics Automatic models trade on the secondary market at prices as low as $313-$490, with mid-range references consistently available between $600-$800 on Chrono24 and similar platforms. A buyer comfortable with pre-owned can access FC’s finishing quality, sapphire crystals, and in some cases genuine manufacture movements for well under $1,000 — competing directly with Tissot’s new-watch lineup.

The Tissot PRX’s 80-hour power reserve is a genuine practical advantage. Set it down on a Friday, pick it up Monday, it’s still running. Watches with automatic movements at the $700-$1,000 bracket typically hit accuracy within +/-10 seconds per day, and both brands land comfortably in that range.

Frederique Constant invests heavily in in-house movement development, and several models use movements designed and built by the brand itself — an appeal for enthusiasts who care about mechanical craftsmanship. The catch: the Sellita SW200-based movements in the entry Classics line are shared with dozens of other brands. You’re paying for the case, dial, and FC name, not a unique calibre. That’s fine if you love the watch. Just go in with clear eyes.

Which one looks better on the wrist?

A question we hear often: “Will a Frederique Constant look out of place next to a Tissot?”

These two brands occupy genuinely different aesthetic territories. The PRX is one of the most talked-about integrated-bracelet watches under $1,000, full stop. The Le Locle leans classical but stays accessible. Both reward everyday wear without demanding much from the owner.

Frederique Constant leans toward a more traditional aesthetic: clean dials, Roman numerals, guilloche-style patterns, classic proportions. If your primary context is formal occasions, client meetings, or pairing with a suit, the Classics collection fits that brief without effort. It has no real sports or integrated-bracelet identity, and that’s a deliberate trade-off, not an oversight.

If you need one watch to cover casual Fridays and Saturday trail runs as well as a black-tie dinner, Tissot wins by a wide margin. If you want a single refined dress watch and already own something more casual separately, FC competes on merit.

What about long-term ownership — service, parts, resale?

Readers frequently ask: “If I buy a Frederique Constant, can I actually get it serviced easily?”

Tissot benefits from Swatch Group’s service infrastructure and spare parts availability, which makes long-term ownership considerably simpler, especially outside Europe. A Tissot can be serviced at authorised dealers in most major cities globally, and movement parts for ETA and Sellita-derived calibres are widely stocked. That is a real and underrated advantage.

Frederique Constant’s service network is thinner. The brand operates out of Geneva and has authorised service centres in major markets, but availability is patchier. For a Sellita-based movement, any competent independent watchmaker can handle the job — the architecture is familiar. For genuine FC manufacture calibres, you are more reliant on brand service, which typically means longer turnaround times and higher costs.

On resale: Tissot watches hold their value reasonably well for the entry-level Swiss segment, with models like the PRX Powermatic 80 retaining 65%-75% of retail value on the secondary market. Frederique Constant’s resale picture is more mixed. The steep discount between new retail and secondary market prices hurts buyers who bought new, but creates real opportunity for anyone entering pre-owned.

Industry estimates put Frederique Constant’s annual production at around 64,000 watches, which is meaningful but not massive volume. The brand isn’t going anywhere, but its market presence is smaller than Tissot’s by a significant factor — worth knowing for long-term parts and service confidence.

So which one should I actually buy?

The honest verdict depends on what you’re solving for.

Buy Tissot if: you want a new watch, you need versatility across casual and dressy contexts, you value a long 80-hour power reserve, or you want an easy service experience anywhere in the world. The PRX Powermatic 80 at ~$625 new is close to a perfect all-rounder for the budget. The Le Locle at ~$495 is the strongest classical dress watch Tissot makes at its price. Neither decision requires much second-guessing.

Buy Frederique Constant if: your primary use case is dress and formal wear, you are comfortable buying pre-owned (where the value proposition dramatically improves), and you want finishing details — perlage on movement bridges, guilloche on the dial, blued screws through the caseback — that Tissot simply does not offer at comparable prices. FC models often feature slimmer profiles and higher-grade finishing visible through the display back. For some buyers, those details matter enough to seek them out specifically.

Before finalising either purchase, it’s worth checking your existing collection against the new piece. Duplicate styles and redundant sizes are easy to miss until a watch arrives. FAQs

Is Frederique Constant better than Tissot? Neither is universally better. Tissot leads on range, value, and everyday versatility. Frederique Constant edges ahead on classical finishing and movement decoration, but only rewards buyers who appreciate those details enough to pay for them.

Is Frederique Constant worth buying under $1,000? Yes, but primarily pre-owned. New Classics Automatic models retail at $1,295-$1,695, but pre-owned examples routinely trade between $400-$800 on Chrono24 and eBay, putting genuine Swiss dress-watch refinement well within a $1,000 budget.

Does Frederique Constant use in-house movements? Some do, some don’t. The Manufacture and Heart Beat lines use proprietary FC calibres. Several Classics and Slimline models run on Sellita SW200-based movements, which are reliable but not unique to the brand.

Which Tissot models compete most directly with Frederique Constant? The Tissot Le Locle and Tissot Gentleman sit closest in style. The PRX Powermatic 80 overlaps on price but is a sportier integrated-bracelet watch — a different aesthetic entirely.

Do Tissot watches hold their value better than Frederique Constant? Tissot models like the PRX Powermatic 80 retain roughly 65%-75% of retail value on the secondary market. Frederique Constant Classics can trade at steep discounts to retail new, but that also makes them attractive pre-owned buys for value-minded collectors.
