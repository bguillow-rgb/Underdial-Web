Is Longines a Good Watch Brand? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published October 1, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes, Longines is a genuinely good watch brand -- Swiss-made, 190+ years of history, solid finishing, and reliable movements at $600-$1,000. It's one of the strongest value propositions in accessible Swiss watchmaking. The honest caveat: you're paying partly for heritage, not hardware you couldn't find elsewhere at this price.

Longines comes up constantly in watch forums, Reddit threads, and gift-buying conversations, and almost always for the same reason: it sits in that intriguing middle zone where “affordable” ends and “luxury” starts to mean something. Before you spend $700-$1,000 on one, here’s an honest editorial take on what you’re actually getting.

So, is Longines actually a good brand — or is it just marketing?

The short answer is yes, genuinely good, with clear-eyed caveats. Longines has over 190 years of watchmaking history, and that heritage isn’t empty: the brand has real roots in precision timekeeping, particularly in sports timing. The company’s history is rooted in sports timekeeping, and the precision applied to its stopwatches can also be found in its wristwatches. That continuity of craft matters at this price tier.

What you get for your money at Longines is notably better case finishing than brands like Tissot or Hamilton — crisper edges, more refined bracelet construction, and dials that read as genuinely upscale rather than merely competent. Longines leads on finishing quality and brand prestige at the $500-$1,000 tier. The honest counterpoint: the movements inside most Longines at this price point are ETA-based rather than proprietary, meaning the engine is shared across multiple Swiss brands. While Longines has mastered the art of optimizing and modifying proven Swiss movements for reliability and value, it’s the case and dial — not some secret movement technology — that you’re really paying a premium for.

How does Longines fit into the Swiss brand hierarchy?

A question we hear often: where Longines actually lands on the brand ladder confuses a lot of buyers, especially because the brand is marketed inconsistently across markets.

Whether Longines is an expensive watch is a matter of opinion — it is a luxury maker, but its prices are slightly more accessible than some competitors. As a result, some people refer to Longines as mid-range luxury. That’s a fair label. Within the Swatch Group’s own lineup (which includes both Tissot and Longines), Longines is positioned above Tissot and below Omega, and that hierarchy shows up in real differences in finishing and materials rather than just marketing language. Case finishing, bracelet construction, brand heritage, and design language are the real differentiators, and these are worth examining closely before any purchase decision.

For a buyer with $800 to spend, the practical question is whether those finishing differences justify a $200-$300 premium over a Tissot PRX Powermatic 80. For many buyers, the answer is a genuine yes, especially if wearing the watch matters as much as owning it. The step up in tactile quality is perceptible.

Is Longines worth buying under $1,000 specifically?

Readers frequently ask: whether Longines actually has good options in the sub-$1,000 range, or whether its strong models all sit above that threshold.

There are solid Longines options at this budget, but you need to know where to look. The HydroConquest — a proper 300m dive watch with sapphire crystal and an ETA-based automatic — sits in the $700-$950 range depending on dial and bracelet configuration, and represents the brand’s strongest value proposition at this tier. The latest iterations of the HydroConquest line strike an impressive balance between design, performance, and value. The Conquest Classic and Spirit lines also dip into the sub-$1,000 zone in certain configurations.

What you don’t get under $1,000 is Longines’ higher-end complications or the more elaborate finishing found on the Master Collection — those models typically start above $1,200. If your heart is set on a Longines Master, buying pre-owned is one of the smarter moves in this vertical. If you want a watch that’s top shelf but at a discount, look at lightly used or like-new condition Longines.

Does Longines hold its value?

This one comes up a lot: buyers increasingly ask whether a Longines purchase makes financial sense beyond the wearing experience.

The straightforward answer is no, not in the way many buyers hope. Longines is a brand where the watch itself represents good value for quality, movement, construction, and brand heritage, but the moment you open the box, it depreciates noticeably. That’s not a knock against Longines specifically — it’s true of nearly all watches under $2,000. The secondary market for Longines is liquid (easy to sell) but not appreciating. Watches are not investments, so don’t believe dealers who try to get you to buy them as such.

The practical upside of this: if you want to buy new, that’s great, and they’re still excellent watches — the value there is in the wearing experience, not so much the sticker cost. For buyers who care about wearing experience over financial return, Longines is easy to recommend. For anyone hoping to buy and flip at a premium in three years, nearly any watch under $1,000 (Longines included) is the wrong tool for that goal.

What should I actually look for if I’m buying a Longines?

When evaluating a specific Longines model — new or pre-owned — focus on four things:

Movement variant. Most Longines at this tier run ETA 2824/2892-based calibers or the in-house L888 (used in the Spirit and some Conquest models). The L888 features a 64-hour power reserve and silicon balance spring, which is a meaningful upgrade worth seeking out if you’re near the top of your budget. Modern automatic movements from ETA (used by Tissot and Longines) are exceptionally reliable, and watches with automatic movements at the $700-$1,000 bracket have accuracy specifications within +/-10 seconds per day — performance that rivals significantly more expensive watches.

Crystal and water resistance. Sapphire crystal is standard across the Longines lineup at this price tier, which is the right expectation. Confirm the water resistance rating matches your lifestyle — the HydroConquest’s 300m rating is meaningfully more capable than a 50m-rated dress watch for anyone who swims or travels actively.

Bracelet quality. This is where Longines earns its price premium most clearly. The metal bracelets on Longines are noticeably more substantial than those on Tissot or Hamilton equivalents — tighter tolerances, better finishing on the links, and more secure clasps. Inspect this closely when buying pre-owned.

Wrist fit. Longines tends toward 38-41mm case diameters with moderate lug-to-lug spans, which works well on a wide range of wrist sizes. If you’re unsure whether a specific reference will sit right, Underdial’s wrist-fit check can cross-reference the case dimensions against your actual measurements before you commit.

Is Longines the right first Swiss watch?

For a buyer choosing their first serious Swiss mechanical watch, Longines is a credible — if slightly premium — entry point. The best first Swiss watch under $1,000 depends on occasion and identity. For a statement piece with cultural recognition, the Tissot PRX Powermatic 80 is strong. For genuine heritage and character, the Hamilton Khaki Field is compelling. Longines sits above both in finish and perceived prestige, but also costs more for what are often mechanically similar packages.

If your budget comfortably reaches $800-$900 and heritage matters to you, Longines is the right call. If you’re stretching to hit that range, Tissot or Hamilton likely deliver 85-90% of the experience at 70% of the cost.

FAQs

Is Longines considered a luxury brand? Longines sits at the lower tier of genuine Swiss luxury — above everyday brands like Tissot and Hamilton, but well below Rolex or Omega. Most collectors call it “accessible luxury” or “mid-range luxury.” The Swiss Made credential and 190+ year history are real, but the movements at this price tier are ETA-based rather than true manufacture calibers.

Do Longines watches hold their value? Mostly no — like most watches under $2,000, Longines models depreciate after purchase, often losing 20-35% in the first few years on the secondary market. The exception is certain discontinued references that develop collector followings. Buy Longines to wear and enjoy, not as an investment.

What is the best Longines watch under $1,000? The Longines HydroConquest (around $700-$950 depending on configuration) is the most compelling Longines under $1,000: solid dive credentials, sapphire crystal, ETA-based automatic movement, and a versatile look that works dressed up or down. The Conquest Classic is worth considering if you want a dress-leaning option in this range.

Is Longines better than Tissot? It depends on what “better” means. Longines generally offers higher-grade case finishing, more refined design language, and stronger brand heritage than Tissot. Tissot competes hard on value — the PRX Powermatic 80 in particular punches close to Longines quality at a lower price point. If budget allows, Longines’ finishing edge is real but narrow.

Should I buy Longines new or pre-owned? Pre-owned is often the smarter move. Longines watches depreciate noticeably after purchase, so a lightly used example from a reputable reseller can deliver the same wearing experience at 25-40% less cost. Focus on finding a clean dial, functioning crown, and ideally original bracelet. Underdial’s pre-owned check feature helps you spot whether a used price is actually fair.

How does Longines compare to Oris at similar prices? Oris starts higher (typically $900+) but offers genuine in-house movement development and a strongly independent identity within Swiss watchmaking. At equivalent prices, Oris edges Longines on movement originality; Longines edges Oris on brand heritage depth and dial finishing refinement. Both are honest, worthwhile purchases — the choice comes down to whether you value independence or heritage more.
