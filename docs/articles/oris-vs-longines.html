Oris vs Longines: Which Swiss Watch Should You Buy?

By Underdial · Published August 23, 2026

Quick Answer

Oris is the better pick if you want an all-mechanical Swiss watch with strong enthusiast credibility and don't mind spending $1,500-$3,000. Longines wins on style variety and value under $1,000, especially for dress and everyday wear. Neither is a bad buy -- they just serve different buyers.

Two names come up constantly when watch buyers start pushing past the Japanese brands but aren’t ready to spend Omega money: Oris and Longines. One is an independent Swiss house that makes mechanical watches and nothing else. The other is one of the oldest names in Swiss watchmaking, now part of the Swatch Group, with styles running from $500 quartz dress pieces to $2,500+ mechanical sport watches. Both are genuinely good. But they’re built for very different buyers, and that distinction matters a lot when you’re working under a $1,000 budget.

What’s the real difference between Oris and Longines?

The accessible Swiss watch market gives you two brands taking fundamentally different roads. Oris is fiercely independent and focused entirely on mechanical watchmaking. Longines is a Swatch Group member with access to vast manufacturing resources. Both deliver genuine Swiss quality at similar price points, yet their philosophies, movements, and identities diverge enough that buying the wrong one for yourself is a real risk.

The short version: Oris leans sporty and mechanical; Longines leans elegant and varied. Oris will never sell you a quartz watch — that’s a philosophical commitment baked into the company. Longines offers both. That distinction shapes everything downstream: which collections exist, which movements you get, which buyers each brand attracts, and where the price floors and ceilings sit.

Which brand actually fits a budget under $1,000?

A question we hear often: this is where the two brands diverge most sharply for practical buyers.

Longines is clearly the more accessible brand at this price. Entry models — particularly the Longines Elegant and some Conquest quartz pieces — start in the $500-$800 window at retail. The upper end stretches to $1,500-$5,000, but the entry tier is genuinely reachable. Longines mechanical watches often feature complications like chronographs and moon phases using ETA calibers exclusive to the brand, and their quartz pieces are accurate and reliable at prices that don’t require a long internal debate.

Oris is a harder fit under $1,000 new. The range runs from roughly 100 to 13,000 euros, but most of the models buyers actually want — Aquis, Divers Sixty-Five, Big Crown — start at $1,200-$1,800 retail. That said, the pre-owned market changes the equation significantly. Clean, lightly used Oris pieces turn up for $700-$950 without much trouble, and that’s a legitimate path. What do you actually get from Oris’s movements?

This one comes up a lot: buyers want to know whether Oris’s mechanical-only stance means a meaningfully better movement, or just a philosophy.

The honest answer is: it depends on which Oris you buy. Entry models use modified Sellita SW200 or ETA 2824-2 movements — solid, proven, and serviceable anywhere in the world, but not exclusive to Oris. Where things get genuinely interesting is the Calibre 400 series. Oris’s flagship Calibre 400 delivers a 5-day (120-hour) power reserve, antimagnetic construction to 2,250 gauss, and a 10-year recommended service interval — specs that rival watches costing three to four times as much.

The trade-off is price. Models featuring Calibre 400 cost approximately $2,500-$3,500, well above the under-$1,000 ceiling we’re working with here, though remarkable value at that tier. For buyers in our price range, the Oris movement story is less dramatic: you’re getting a well-regulated Swiss automatic, not a technical tour de force. That’s still meaningful, but keep expectations grounded.

Does Longines have a movement quality problem?

Swatch Group membership brings economies of scale that reduce production costs, which is a big part of why Longines can price so competitively. The flip side is that Longines base calibers appear, with modifications, across multiple Swatch Group brands, which reduces exclusivity.

Is that a real problem? For most buyers, no. A $600 Tissot keeps time about as well as a $3,000 Tudor — the same logic applies here. Longines movements are accurate, reliable, and fully serviceable. What you’re not getting is uniqueness. If knowing that the base caliber in your Longines HydroConquest is related to movements found in Mido or Tissot bothers you, Oris is a better psychological fit. If it doesn’t, Longines offers more watch for less money.

Which brand has better brand recognition — and does that matter?

Readers frequently ask: will people know what I’m wearing?

Longines wins this one. There’s a good chance more people will recognize the watch on your wrist compared to an Oris. The name carries genuine recognition outside watch circles — it’s been a sponsor of Wimbledon, the Olympics, and major equestrian events for decades. Non-watch people will register “Swiss, classic, looks expensive” when they see a Longines. That’s worth something in certain contexts.

Oris recognition is largely confined to the enthusiast community. Most people who don’t follow watches will read it as a vaguely Swiss-sounding unknown. That cuts both ways. If you find status signaling tiresome, Oris’s relative anonymity is a feature. If you’re buying partly because of how a watch reads in professional settings, Longines wins easily.

Which holds its value better on the secondhand market?

Neither brand should be bought as an investment, full stop. Both Oris and Longines hold their ground reasonably well, but significant appreciation is not something to count on.

Oris watches, particularly discontinued models, can fetch strong prices on the secondhand market. Rarer Longines editions also command higher prices. For standard models of either brand, though, depreciation is the rule.

The practical takeaway: standard Longines dress models depreciate fairly quickly after purchase — great news if you’re buying pre-owned, less great if you’re buying new and expect to recoup much. Oris sport models (Aquis, Divers Sixty-Five) tend to hold secondary values somewhat better, partly because of their enthusiast following and partly because Oris’s independent status creates genuine scarcity on some references. For buyers in the under-$1,000 band buying pre-owned, Longines offers more available inventory; Oris offers better narrative and potentially better holds on popular references.

What are the best models from each brand to consider?

For Longines under $1,000 (new or pre-owned):

  • Longines Elegant Quartz (~$550-$700 new): slim, sapphire crystal, reliable quartz movement, excellent dress watch value
  • Longines La Grande Classique (~$600-$850 new): one of the cleanest Swiss dress watches at this price; exceptionally legible
  • Longines Conquest Heritage (pre-owned, ~$700-$950): mechanical, vintage-inspired, better bracelet quality than Longines’s entry tier

For Oris under $1,000 (pre-owned focus):

  • Oris Big Crown Pointer Date (pre-owned ~$750-$950): distinctive retrograde date display, in-house-era feel, genuinely characterful
  • Oris Aquis Date (pre-owned ~$800-$1,000): 300m water resistance, solid bracelet, the brand’s most recognizable sport model
  • Oris Classic Date (pre-owned ~$600-$800): understated dress-sport crossover, accessible intro to the brand

So which should you actually buy?

The decision comes down to budget ceiling, movement philosophy, and style preference.

If you’re spending new money under $1,000, Longines is the practical answer. The model range is wider, the retail experience is smoother, and the dress-watch value at $600-$800 is hard to beat in Swiss-made territory. The timeless designs and genuine Swiss heritage make it a sensible starting point for anyone building a collection.

If you’re willing to buy pre-owned and stretch to $1,000, or if you simply want a watch from a brand that makes only mechanical movements and prizes engineering independence, Oris earns the nod. The commitment to mechanical watchmaking is real, not marketing, and the quality shows even on entry references. For enthusiasts who know what they want, it’s a compelling choice.

Neither is wrong. They’re only wrong if you pick the brand that doesn’t match what you actually want from a watch.

FAQs

Is Oris a prestigious watch brand? Yes, Oris is respected in the watch community as a fiercely independent Swiss maker committed to mechanical-only movements. It’s not a household name like Rolex, but enthusiasts regard it highly for quality and engineering honesty.

Is Longines considered a luxury watch? Longines sits in the accessible Swiss luxury tier — genuinely Swiss-made with real heritage, but priced well below luxury houses like Omega or IWC. Entry models start around $500-$800, making it achievable for serious buyers under $1,000.

Does Longines hold its value better than Oris? Neither brand holds value particularly well as everyday buys — expect 30%-50% depreciation on standard models. Oris discontinued references and special editions can command premiums on the secondhand market, giving Oris a slight edge for collectors.

Can I find a good Oris watch under $1,000? New Oris watches start around $1,200-$1,500 for entry models, putting them above most buyers’ $1,000 ceiling. Pre-owned, you can find solid Oris pieces for $700-$950 — a legitimate option Underdial can help you cross-check against your wrist size and collection.

Which brand is better for a first serious Swiss watch? Longines is usually the smarter first Swiss watch under $1,000 — wider model range, strong AD support, and proven resale liquidity. Graduate to Oris when you know exactly which style and complication you want, and are ready to spend $1,500+ new.
