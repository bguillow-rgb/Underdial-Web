Is Swiss Made Worth It? An Honest Answer for Buyers

By Underdial · Published October 19, 2026

Quick Answer

Swiss Made is legally meaningful, a watch must have a Swiss movement, be assembled in Switzerland, and pass Swiss quality inspection. Under $1,000, brands like Tissot, Hamilton, and Certina deliver genuine value behind the label. But it's not automatically better than Japanese-made at the same price; what matters is the specific watch, not the stamp alone.

The two words “Swiss Made” appear on millions of watch dials, carry centuries of reputation behind them, and add a real premium to the price tag. But if you’re weighing a Tissot against a Seiko, or wondering whether to stretch your budget to hit the Swiss threshold, you deserve a straight answer: does that stamp actually mean something, or is it mostly marketing?

The short version: it means something real, but not everything people assume it means. Here’s a full breakdown.

What exactly does Swiss Made mean on a watch dial?

A question we hear often: this one sounds simple, but most buyers have never seen the actual legal definition — and it’s stricter than you’d expect.

Since January 1, 2017, a watch can legally bear the Swiss Made label only if it meets three criteria: the movement must be Swiss, the watch must be assembled in Switzerland, and it must pass Swiss quality inspection. On top of those requirements, Swiss law requires that at least 60% of a watch’s total manufacturing costs originate in Switzerland. That last threshold was a significant tightening from the older, looser standard that let brands put “Swiss Made” on products with only a token Swiss component.

Misuse of the label is a criminal offense under Swiss law — not just a regulatory fine. So when you see it on a Tissot or Hamilton dial, a government-backed framework is behind it, not just a brand claim.

What the label does not guarantee: that the movement is in-house, that the watch will outlast a Japanese alternative, or that it’s the best option at its price point. Those are separate questions, and important ones.

Is the Swiss Made label worth paying extra for?

This one comes up a lot: someone is deciding between a ~$250 Seiko and a ~$450 Tissot and wants to know if the gap is justified by the label or just by prestige.

The honest answer is that Swiss Made is worth it for some reasons, not for every reason people imagine. It is not magic. It does not turn every watch into a future heirloom. Yet it remains one of the few markings in watchmaking that still carries real weight — culturally, technically, and emotionally.

Practically speaking, the Swiss Made premium at the $400-$1,000 level buys you access to movements sourced from ETA or Sellita, two Swiss companies that supply the bulk of the industry, regulated by Swiss assembly facilities and inspected to consistent standards. At the lower end — Tissot, Hamilton, Certina — you get Swiss movements with demonstrably better regulation and quality control than comparable Asian movements. The accuracy tends to be better. The service network tends to be better.

That said, the premium is real money. A Seiko 5 Sports running the NH38 movement costs under $300 and is an excellent, reliable automatic. A Tissot Gentleman Powermatic 80 at around $425 is also excellent. Neither is a bad choice. The Swiss Made label tips the scales on specifics — movement regulation, finishing consistency, authorized service infrastructure — not on some abstract notion of superiority.

Does Swiss Made actually mean a better movement inside?

Not always, and this is where buyers often get tripped up.

At the $500-$1,000 range, Japanese watches (particularly Seiko and Orient) offer in-house manufactured movements, while most Swiss watches at this price use movements from ETA or Sellita. The “Swiss Made” label adds brand prestige but doesn’t automatically mean superior quality.

To put numbers on it: the Tissot Powermatic 80 movement (found in the PRX, the Gentleman, and others) offers an 80-hour power reserve and a silicon hairspring that resists magnetism — genuinely impressive specs for a shared caliber. But the Seiko NH35 and NH38 movements powering watches like the Seiko 5 Sports are fully manufactured in-house by Seiko. In-house doesn’t automatically win, but it’s a different kind of value proposition. Japanese manufacturers absorb the full cost of movement development, while Swiss brands at entry level draw on a mature, shared-movement supply chain.

The Swiss watch industry is worth over CHF 26 billion a year, making it the largest in the world. That means the supply chain for movements, parts, and servicing is extraordinarily deep, which matters when your watch needs its first service in 5-7 years. Finding an authorized service center for a Tissot or Hamilton is rarely a problem anywhere in the world.

When is Swiss Made not worth the extra spend?

A question we hear often: when should someone skip the Swiss premium and buy Japanese or a microbrand instead?

If you want the lowest-cost way to tell accurate time, a Swiss watch is unnecessary. If you tend to replace accessories as trends shift, its durability and serviceability may not matter much either.

Three situations where the Swiss Made premium doesn’t pay off:

1. You’re under $300 and want an automatic. Below that threshold, there are no genuinely compelling Swiss Made automatics. The Seiko 5 Sports and Orient Mako dominate this tier. Trying to reach Swiss Made at $250 means landing on Swatch’s Sistem51 — fun, but limited in finishing and strap options.

2. You want maximum movement specs per dollar. The NH35/NH38 in Seiko watches and the F6922 in Orient models are produced entirely in-house, which some collectors value more than a shared ETA caliber regardless of national origin.

3. You’re buying a second or third watch in a collection. If you already own a solid Swiss Made piece, a second watch from Seiko, Orient, or a microbrand adds more variety than another Swiss label at a similar spec level. When auditing your collection — something Underdial helps you do by checking new additions against what you already own — diversity often beats duplication.

Does Swiss Made help watches hold their value?

This question deserves a realistic answer rather than hype in either direction.

The truth is, no matter what many publications will have you believe, you will never make your money back on a mechanical watch, and very few actually go up in value. That applies to Swiss Made watches just as much as Japanese ones in the sub-$1,000 bracket. The Tissot PRX Powermatic 80 is a great watch; it is not an investment.

What Swiss Made does tend to provide is slightly better resale stability compared to fashion watches or generic Asian-movement pieces at similar prices. A Swiss watch can earn emotional value because it is designed to stay — repairable in a way most modern accessories are not. Its movement can be serviced, its bracelet refinished. Longevity is not guaranteed, but a well-chosen watch invites long-term ownership rather than constant replacement.

The practical implication: if you sell a Hamilton Khaki Field or Tissot PRX after two years in good condition, you’ll recover a fair portion of your spend. The same is true of a Seiko Presage. Swiss Made is a moderate positive factor in resale, not a decisive one.

How do I actually decide — Swiss Made or not?

The cleanest decision framework we’ve found: decide on the style and spec you want first, then see which watches deliver it and whether any are Swiss Made.

If the spec sheet and design land on a Tissot or Hamilton, the Swiss Made label is a bonus confirming you’re getting consistent manufacturing standards. If the same spec sheet lands on a Seiko Prospex or Orient Star, you don’t need to talk yourself out of it to chase a label.

If you value mechanical tradition, disciplined manufacturing, and the quiet confidence of a watch with genuine provenance, Swiss Made can be deeply worthwhile. But the label should confirm a good decision, not create one.

Before committing either way, it’s worth checking any shortlisted watch against your existing collection and wrist size. FAQs

What does Swiss Made legally require on a watch? Since 2017, a watch must have a Swiss movement, be assembled in Switzerland, and pass quality inspection there. At least 60% of manufacturing costs must originate in Switzerland. The rules tightened significantly from the looser pre-2017 standard.

Is a Swiss Made watch better than a Japanese watch under $1,000? Not automatically. Japanese brands like Seiko and Orient often use fully in-house movements at this price. Swiss brands like Tissot and Hamilton typically use ETA or Sellita movements — excellent calibers, but not proprietary. Both make outstanding watches; the decision should come down to the individual model, not the country of origin.

Which Swiss Made watches under $500 are actually worth buying? The Tissot PRX Powermatic 80 ($475), Tissot Gentleman Powermatic 80 ($425), and Hamilton Khaki Field Automatic (~$495) consistently rank as the strongest Swiss Made value under $500 in 2026. Each offers genuine Swiss assembly, sapphire crystal, and an 80-hour power reserve movement.

Does Swiss Made help a watch hold its value better? Modestly, yes — Swiss heritage brands tend to retain resale value better than comparable no-name or fashion-label watches. However, no watch under $1,000 should be bought as a financial investment; value retention is a secondary benefit, not a reason to buy.

Can a cheap watch claim Swiss Made falsely? Swiss authorities actively enforce the Swiss Made ordinance, and misuse of the label is a criminal offense under Swiss law. That said, the label doesn’t guarantee a great watch — it guarantees a minimum production standard. Always evaluate the specific model on its own specs and reputation.
