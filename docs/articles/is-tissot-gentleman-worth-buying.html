Is the Tissot Gentleman Worth Buying? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published November 21, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes, the Tissot Gentleman is worth buying for most buyers under $1,000. It pairs a proven 80-hour Powermatic 80 movement with sapphire crystal and versatile Swiss styling, all for around $850. It's not flashy, but it's one of the most complete everyday automatics at this price.

The Tissot Gentleman sits in a quietly crowded corner of the market: the sub-$900 Swiss everyday automatic, where it’s easy for a watch to get lost. And yet reviewers, forum regulars, and long-term owners keep coming back to it. Here’s what it actually offers, where it falls short, and who should genuinely buy one.

Okay, so is it actually worth the money?

The short answer: yes, for most buyers in this price range.

The Tissot Gentleman is not a tool-watch icon like a dive watch, nor a fragile dress piece reserved for black-tie occasions. Its appeal comes from a carefully judged middle ground that makes it one of Tissot’s most sensible entries for buyers picking up their first Swiss automatic or upgrading from fashion watches.

At roughly $850 retail you get a Swiss-made automatic, sapphire crystal, solid water resistance, and a movement that frankly over-delivers for the price. Proven caliber, long power reserve, finishing that looks appropriate in professional settings. That combination is genuinely rare below $1,000.

The watch isn’t trying to be flashy. If the PRX grabs attention from across the room, the Gentleman is the one that slowly wins people over the longer they spend with it: understated, mature, and a little overlooked within the wider Tissot catalogue. For some buyers, that’s exactly the point.

What does the Powermatic 80 movement actually give you?

A question we hear often: does the movement justify the price premium over a quartz Tissot or a cheaper Japanese automatic?

The Tissot Gentleman Powermatic 80 runs the ETA Powermatic 80 caliber, with 80 hours of power reserve and a Nivachron balance spring. It’s one of the Swatch Group’s workhorse movements. No fancy finishing, but one of the most proven calibers in the business.

Eighty hours of reserve is the practical number to care about. Most entry automatics offer 38-42 hours, meaning a watch left on the nightstand Friday night is dead by Sunday. With the Gentleman, you can skip the weekend and re-sync on Tuesday. That’s not a minor feature. It’s the reason regular wearers rarely think about winding.

Some long-term owners report accuracy near chronometer grade in real-world wear. Independent testing tends to back this up: many Powermatic 80 examples run within plus or minus 5 seconds per day, well inside the plus or minus 15 seconds per day COSC threshold, without carrying the premium that official certification adds.

For comparison, Seiko’s NH35 (found in watches like the Seiko 5 Sports around $280) offers roughly 41 hours of reserve and no anti-magnetic protection. The Powermatic 80’s 80-hour reserve and Nivachron spring are a meaningful step up, and they’re part of what makes the Gentleman’s price defensible.

What size should I get: the new 38mm or the original 40mm?

This one comes up a lot: Tissot quietly introduced the Gentleman in 38mm in 2026, and it’s already shifting how buyers approach the line.

The Tissot Gentleman had become a reliable recommendation for attainable everyday carry, with one persistent problem: sizing. The original 40mm works well on medium-to-large wrists, but many buyers drawn to its dress-watch character found it slightly oversized. The 38mm is, honestly, the size the Gentleman was always meant to be. A few millimeters matter more than people expect.

Practically speaking: if your wrist measures under 17cm, the 38mm is the smarter buy. The case sits flatter and the proportions read closer to a true dress watch. On larger wrists (18-19cm and up), the 40mm fills the space better. Both sizes run the same Powermatic 80 movement, so the core value proposition doesn’t change. All four models of the Tissot Gentleman 38mm are priced at $850, matching the 40mm, so the choice is purely about wrist fit and aesthetics.

The PRX wins on value-per-dollar (sapphire, 80-hour reserve, integrated bracelet, $675), and it’s the more eye-catching of the two. But its sportier integrated bracelet makes it harder to dress up. If your context is client meetings, formal events, or conservative professional environments, the Gentleman’s separate-lug case swaps to a leather strap far more naturally. Are there any real downsides I should know about?

Readers frequently ask: what do owners actually complain about?

Two consistent gripes come up. First, the lug width. The 40mm’s 21mm lugs are an awkward middle ground: most aftermarket strap makers stock 20mm and 22mm, so sourcing takes more effort. The 38mm switches to 18mm, which drew its own complaints from buyers who expected 20mm given how many quality straps are built around that size. Neither is a dealbreaker, but if you’re a strap-changer, budget extra time for sourcing.

Second, the price gap versus the PRX. At $850 against the PRX’s roughly $675, the Gentleman costs about $175 more for a watch many casual observers won’t distinguish. The premium buys a quieter aesthetic, a more strap-friendly case, and better dress versatility. Worthwhile if those things matter to you, less so if you’re buying on spec sheet alone.

What you won’t find real complaints about: the Tissot Gentleman Powermatic 80 holds 547 ratings averaging 4.6 stars across multiple roundups. That’s a sample size that carries real weight.

Who should actually buy this watch?

A buyer who needs a watch for client meetings, weddings, and daily office life will often get more mileage from the Gentleman than from a sportier watch or a true dress piece with low water resistance.

If you’re buying your first automatic and want something you can wear every day without thinking about it, the Gentleman Automatic is the top recommendation across multiple independent reviews. That consistency from reviewers with no stake in the sale matters.

It’s one of the stronger contenders for a “one-watch collection”: a reliable movement, 170 years of Swiss heritage, and a level of refinement that consistently outpaces competitors at the same price.

Buy it if: you want one Swiss automatic that covers office, smart-casual, and occasional formal wear; you prefer understated design over bold integrated bracelets; you want 80 hours of reserve; your wrist suits 38mm or 40mm.

Look elsewhere if: you want a dedicated diver or field watch; you’re prioritizing raw value-per-dollar (the PRX gives you more watch for less money); you need COSC certification for precision-critical use; or you’re a heavy strap-swapper frustrated by non-standard lug widths.

What movement does the Tissot Gentleman use?
