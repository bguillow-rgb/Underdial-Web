How to Wind a Watch: Automatic & Manual, Done Right

By Underdial · Published September 2, 2026

Quick Answer

To wind a manual watch, remove it from your wrist, turn the crown clockwise 20-40 turns until you feel firm resistance, then push the crown back in. For an automatic, 30-40 clockwise turns off the wrist gets it running -- your arm does the rest throughout the day.

Most people buying their first mechanical watch (a Seiko 5, an Orient Bambino, a Hamilton Khaki) never get told the basics of winding one. The watch arrives, looks great, runs for a day, then stops. The new owner is baffled. This guide answers every question that follows.

Do I actually need to wind an automatic watch, or does it just run by itself?

A question we hear often:

Automatic watches are self-winding, up to a point. A rotor moves with your wrist and transfers energy to the mainspring. During an active day, the watch winds itself continuously. Simple enough.

But two situations call for manual winding: when the watch has stopped after sitting unworn for a few days, and when you want maximum power reserve before a day where you’ll mostly be sitting still. Some people also give their automatic a few turns each morning after leaving it on the nightstand overnight.

The practical rule is this: if the seconds hand is moving, wear it normally. If the watch has stopped, wind it first, then put it on. Trying to set the time on a dead automatic without winding it is a common mistake. Wind it first to get it running, then set the time. Setting the time on a stopped movement can engage the date-change mechanism at the wrong position, which can damage the date wheel.

What’s the right way to wind an automatic watch without breaking anything?

The process is simpler than the anxiety around it. Here’s the full sequence.

Step 1 — Take it off your wrist. Always wind off the wrist. Winding while wearing the watch applies lateral pressure to the crown stem at an angle, and over thousands of winding sessions that can bend the stem, resulting in a $100-$300 repair. The 10 seconds of extra effort is worth it.

Step 2 — Check for a screw-down crown. Many dive and tool watches (Seiko Prospex models, Orient Kamasu, and similar) use a screw-down crown for water resistance. If yours has one, unscrew it gently counterclockwise until it pops out to the winding position.

Step 3 — Wind clockwise, 30-40 turns. Turn smoothly, no need to rush. You’ll feel resistance building, and most modern automatics have a slipping-clutch that prevents overwinding, so you’re not going to hurt anything.

Step 4 — Push the crown back in (and re-tighten if it’s screw-down). For screw-down crowns, take a moment to thread it back properly. A crown that isn’t fully seated is a path for dust and moisture.

How do you wind a manual (hand-wound) watch? Is it different?

This one comes up a lot:

The technique is similar, but two things are different: manual movements need daily winding, and they have a genuine hard stop you should not force past.

A manual watch has no rotor. The mainspring winds up by hand and gradually unwinds to power the movement. Most manual calibers offer 40-48 hours of power reserve, so winding every morning is the right habit to build.

To wind: remove the watch, hold it face-up in your non-dominant hand, and turn the crown clockwise. You’ll feel slight resistance that increases as tension builds. Somewhere in the range of 20-40 turns, that resistance becomes firm and consistent. That’s your stop point. Unlike modern automatics, hand-wound movements have a true mechanical limit, and forcing past it can crack the mainspring or strip gears. When the resistance is firm and steady, stop. Push the crown back in and you’re done.

Vintage hand-wound movements (pre-1980 Hamilton, Seiko, or Citizen calibers) deserve extra caution. They typically need fewer turns than modern calibers: often 20-25 rather than 35-40. Go slowly and pay attention to what you’re feeling through the crown.

Can you overwind a watch and actually break it?

Readers frequently ask:

For modern automatics: no. When the mainspring is fully wound, the slipping clutch disengages and the crown turns freely without adding tension. You physically cannot break a modern automatic by winding too much. That said, repeatedly winding well past the slip point does add wear to the clutch over many years, so the sensible approach is 30-40 turns and stop.

For manual (hand-wound) movements, the calculus is different. The hard stop is real, and forcing past it is how you crack a mainspring. See the section above.

When’s the best time of day to wind a watch?

Morning is the near-universal recommendation, and there’s practical logic behind it. No winding happens while the watch sits on the nightstand overnight, so starting the day with 20-30 turns before you put it on means you’re never scrambling to top up the reserve mid-afternoon. Build it into the same routine as putting the watch on and it becomes automatic.

For automatics in the 24-48 hour power reserve range (the Orient calibers, the Miyota 8215, and similar budget workhorses), that quick morning wind is all you really need. Your arm activity during the day handles the rest.

One timing note worth knowing: if your watch reads somewhere between 10:00 pm and 2:00 am, don’t change the date manually. The movement is mid-cycle on the date change, and forcing it can damage the date mechanism. Wind and wear the watch first, then set the date once the hands have cleared the midnight window.

What about winding direction — does clockwise vs. counterclockwise matter?

For crown-winding, always clockwise. Turning counterclockwise doesn’t engage the winding mechanism. It won’t cause damage, but it won’t do anything useful either.

For motorized watch winders, the answer is more nuanced. Most modern calibers use a bidirectional winding system, meaning the rotor winds the spring on both clockwise and counterclockwise rotation. For those watches the winder direction setting is effectively irrelevant. Bidirectional covers you either way.

A handful of movements (Patek Philippe, A. Lange & Sohne, and certain Seiko Spring Drive calibers) wind in one direction only, and the wrong winder setting means the rotor spins without engaging the winding mechanism at all. At the under-$1,000 price point, though, the Japanese automatics (NH35, NH38, the Miyota family) all wind bidirectionally, so this is largely a non-issue for most buyers here.

Any environmental traps to avoid when winding?

Wind in a clean, dry spot away from strong magnetic fields. Speakers, laptops, and magnetic phone mounts can magnetize the balance spring, causing dramatic timing errors. Dust and moisture are also worth thinking about when the crown is out, since an open crown is an open door.

Also worth noting: a loose crown usually means a worn crown tube or damaged threading. If the crown feels loose or wobbly, stop winding and take it to a watchmaker. Continuing to wind through a loose crown risks detaching the stem or letting moisture into the case.

Automatics left sitting for more than 48 hours typically need a manual wind before wearing again, and catching that before you leave the house saves the minor frustration of setting the time in the car.

FAQs

Can you overwind an automatic watch? No. Modern automatic watches have a slipping-clutch mechanism that disengages when the mainspring is fully wound, so the crown just turns freely without adding tension. You cannot break the spring by winding too much, but repeatedly winding past the slip point does add unnecessary wear to the clutch over years.

Should you wind a watch while wearing it? No. Winding on the wrist puts lateral pressure on the crown stem at an angle, which can gradually bend the stem over thousands of cycles. Always remove the watch first, hold it in your non-dominant hand with the crown facing you, and wind from there.

How often do you need to wind a manual watch? Daily, ideally at the same time each morning. Most manual movements have a 40-48 hour power reserve, so skipping a day will stop the watch. Consistent daily winding at low tension keeps the mainspring in its optimal operating zone.

How many turns does it take to fully wind a watch? Roughly 20-40 crown turns depending on the caliber. Vintage movements may feel fully wound after 20-25 turns; modern automatic calibers like the NH35 or Miyota 9015 typically take 30-40 turns. Stop when you feel steady resistance, and never force past it.

Is it bad to let an automatic watch run down completely? Occasionally letting it run down is harmless. The concern is that modern watch lubricants can pool and settle when the movement sits completely still for extended periods (weeks or months, not a day or two). For everyday watches, the bigger risk is simply that you lose your time-set and have to recalibrate.
