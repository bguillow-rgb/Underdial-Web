Is Christopher Ward a Good Watch Brand? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published October 31, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes. Christopher Ward is a genuinely good watch brand, British-founded, Swiss-made, and built around a direct-to-consumer model that cuts out the dealer markup. You get real sapphire crystals, Sellita or in-house movements, and 300 m water resistance at prices that undercut comparably specced Swiss rivals by a meaningful margin.

If you’ve been circling the Christopher Ward website, tab-switching between the C60 Trident and a Tissot Seastar, wondering whether CW is the real deal or just clever marketing, this is the article you need. We break down the brand’s origins, what its watches actually deliver at each price tier, where they fall short, and who they’re best suited to.

Wait, is Christopher Ward actually Swiss Made or just a British brand?

A question we hear often: buyers spot the British branding and assume the watches are made in the UK. The reality is more interesting.

Christopher Ward (London) Limited was founded in 2004 by Christopher Ward, Mike France, and Peter Ellis. The pitch was simple from day one: sell more watch for the money than almost any brand in existence by cutting out every middleman between the Swiss workshop and your wrist. To make that permanent, the company merged with its Swiss manufacturing partner Synergies Horlogeres SA in 2014 to form Christopher Ward London Holdings Ltd. The brand identity is British; the watches are Swiss Made in every legally meaningful sense.

That structure matters at this price bracket. You’re not getting a Swiss-labelled quartz assembled in a Chinese warehouse. You’re getting movements sourced from established Swiss suppliers like Sellita, regulated in-house, and fitted into cases designed in England and manufactured in the Vallee de Joux region.

What do you actually get for the money across the lineup?

The current lineup runs from the sport-focused C63 Sealander at around £595 up to the haute-complication C1 Bel Canto Classic at EUR 4,450, with The Twelve and C60 Trident sitting in between as the brand’s best-known names.

For most buyers in the under-$1,000 bracket, the entry point is the C60 Trident Reef. Prices start from £695/US$850 on a rubber strap, which is good value given what’s inside: 200 m water resistance, a Sellita SW200-1 movement, an anti-reflective sapphire crystal, and a genuine screw-down crown. You’d expect to pay considerably more for that spec list from a Swiss-heritage brand with a retail presence.

Step up slightly and you get the C63 Sealander Automatic, the all-rounder of the range. It answers the question “what if I just want a damn good watch?” It’s a versatile, everyday, jack-of-all-trades sort of piece, and that’s not a criticism, it’s a design philosophy. The watch you grab when you don’t know what you want to wear.

Is the build quality actually as good as the spec sheets suggest?

This one comes up a lot: it’s easy to be sceptical when a brand leads with its value proposition. But the evidence holds up.

The cases have been reproportioned, the dials and hands refined, and the bracelets now feature one of the better tool-free sizing systems reviewers have come across. CW calls it iLink. You adjust links without a spring bar tool, and while CW isn’t the only brand offering that, this is possibly the best implementation available.

Dial and case finishing is genuinely improved, and the iLink system impresses even if it’s not something you’d show off at dinner. On the C60 Trident Lumiere specifically, CW’s lume-focused titanium diver, the first thing you notice is how slim and well-balanced it sits on the wrist at only 10.85 mm thick. In a category where dive watches routinely drift into overbuilt territory, that’s real discipline.

One detail worth noting: Christopher Ward states it regulates the SW200-1 in-house to -5/+12 seconds per day while running at 4 Hz. That beats the standard Sellita rating and is a quality-control step that budget-tier brands typically skip.

Who is Christopher Ward not right for?

Resale value is the honest caveat. Christopher Ward sells direct with no dealer network propping up secondary pricing the way Rolex or Omega enjoy, so expect a real drop from retail on the used market, similar to most independent and microbrand names.

There’s also the try-before-you-buy problem. CW has six showrooms as of 2026, London, Maidenhead, Liverpool, New York, Frisco, and Falls Church. If you’re not near one of those cities, you’re buying from photos and spec sheets. That’s a genuine leap for a watch in the $800-$1,200 range.

Christopher Ward makes sense for a buyer who wants genuine Swiss mechanical engineering, including real in-house calibres at the higher end, without paying for a dealer network or a large marketing budget. It makes less sense for a buyer chasing resale value or the ability to try a reference on before committing.

How does Christopher Ward compare to Tissot, Mido, or Longines at the same money?

Readers frequently ask: where CW actually fits against the Swiss stalwarts.

The honest answer is that CW punches above its price in hardware, sapphire crystals, higher water resistance ratings, and often a more legible dial, while trading below the Swiss establishment brands in two softer currencies: heritage and secondary-market recognition. A Tissot PRX or a Mido Ocean Star carries more immediate name recognition at dinner parties and holds a higher percentage of retail value after three years. Christopher Ward wins on the spec sheet; the Swiss maisons win on cachet.

The tipping point for most buyers in the $700-$1,100 range comes down to what they’ll actually use the watch for. If you want high-quality materials and Swiss-made movements without paying a heritage premium, CW is the logical choice. If the name on the dial matters, it isn’t.

Has the brand changed recently, and is it drifting upmarket?

This is the most important context for 2026 buyers. Christopher Ward still represents real value, but it’s no longer primarily in the segment it once occupied. It’s an accessible-luxury brand now, one a younger collector would have had to eventually step up to rather than start with.

The C60 Trident Biscay, the brand’s retro skin-diver tribute, is priced from £1,330/€1,740/$1,870 on a hybrid strap. That’s a long way from where Christopher Ward started. The entry-level anchor is now the Trident Reef and C63 Sealander range; the brand’s centre of gravity has moved.

That’s not necessarily bad news. There are more compelling sub-$1,000 brands than ever, and it makes sense to see Christopher Ward take on the Swiss luxury brands that once dismissed it. For Underdial readers with a sub-$1,000 ceiling, the sweet spot is the Trident Reef and base Sealander Automatic, where CW’s value proposition is strongest and hasn’t yet been eroded by aspirational pricing.

The verdict: is Christopher Ward actually worth buying?

Christopher Ward sells more watch for the money than almost any brand in existence. That’s the direct result of cutting out every middleman between the Swiss workshop and your wrist. For buyers who care about what’s inside the case more than the prestige of what’s on the dial, that’s a compelling offer.

The caveats are real: limited in-person availability, soft resale value, and a lineup that increasingly nudges past the $1,000 threshold. But at the entry level, around $850-$1,050, Christopher Ward remains one of the most objectively well-specced Swiss-made watches you can buy.

FAQs

Are Christopher Ward watches Swiss Made? Yes. Christopher Ward merged with its Swiss manufacturing partner Synergies Horlogeres in 2014, and all current watches carry a genuine Swiss Made designation with movements assembled and regulated in Switzerland.

Do Christopher Ward watches hold their value? Not especially. As a direct-to-consumer brand with no dealer network propping up secondary-market prices, expect a real depreciation from retail on the used market, broadly similar to other independents and microbrands.

What movement does Christopher Ward use? Most C60 and C63 models run Sellita calibres (SW200-1 or SW330-2 GMT). Higher-end pieces like The Twelve and the Bel Canto use CW’s own in-house Calibre SH21. All are regulated in-house before shipping.

Can I try a Christopher Ward watch before buying? Only in a handful of cities. As of 2026, CW has six showrooms, London, Maidenhead, Liverpool, New York, Frisco, and Falls Church. Outside those locations you’re buying online, though the brand offers a return window.

What is the best Christopher Ward watch under $1,000? The C60 Trident Reef is the strongest value play, starting around £695/$850 on a rubber strap with 200 m water resistance, a sapphire crystal, and a Sellita SW200-1 automatic. It’s one of the most spec-dense dive watches at that price.
