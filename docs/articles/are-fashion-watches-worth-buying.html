Are Fashion Watches Worth Buying? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published September 5, 2026

Quick Answer

Fashion watches from brands like Michael Kors, Daniel Wellington, Fossil, and MVMT are worth buying if you want an affordable, on-trend accessory and have no plans to service or resell it. For the same money, however, brands like Seiko, Citizen, or Tissot deliver better movements, longer lifespans, and significantly more horological value.

Most people shopping for a fashion watch under a few hundred dollars are asking a completely fair question. The watch looks sharp on the wrist, the brand name carries recognition, and the price feels reasonable. So what’s the catch? Here’s an honest breakdown of what fashion watches are, who they make sense for, and where your money goes further.

What exactly counts as a “fashion watch” — and why does the distinction matter?

The term “fashion watch” describes timepieces produced by clothing, accessories, or lifestyle brands — think Michael Kors, Fossil, Daniel Wellington, MVMT, Hugo Boss, Armani Exchange, and Guess — where the design and brand identity drive the purchase, not the movement inside. The Fossil Group manufactures Michael Kors watches and also licenses production for a number of other fashion-house brands. And while the fashion house itself is often called a “luxury brand,” the watches don’t qualify as luxury watches by the standards of the broader watch community.

The distinction matters because it shapes every decision around value, durability, and longevity. A watch built around a movement is designed to be serviced and worn for decades. A watch built around a logo is designed to look good now and be replaced in a few years.

So are fashion watches actually bad watches?

A question we hear often: not exactly — but they’re almost always over-priced for what’s inside.

A common complaint about brands like Daniel Wellington, Guess, or Armani Exchange is their use of low-end Japanese quartz movements, usually the Miyota 1L22, worth about $5-$10. That movement sits inside a case retailing for $150-$300, with the margin going almost entirely to marketing, brand licensing, and distribution. These brands put their logos on mass-produced watches made at very low cost, then sell them at well over $100 each.

That said, the watches do function. They keep reasonable time, they look presentable, and for someone who wants a wrist accessory that matches their wardrobe — without any intention of servicing or keeping it for ten years — they’re not a disaster. A Michael Kors watch may be durable enough for everyday fashion wear but may not hold up under heavy use the way higher-end watches do. The honest problem is that at their actual price points, better alternatives exist in almost every case.

Does the “Fossil Group” angle change anything about Michael Kors or other brands?

Readers frequently ask: whether knowing Fossil makes Michael Kors watches means you could just buy a Fossil instead and save money.

Yes — and that’s actually useful to know. Michael Kors skews toward fashion-forward statement pieces; Fossil leans toward vintage-inspired, everyday designs at a slightly lower price. The aesthetic focus differs, but the underlying economics are similar.

The sharper insight is one level deeper. Seiko and Citizen offer in-house movements with real horological credibility; Fossil’s movements are fashion-oriented. In practical terms: if you’re drawn to the minimalist look of a Fossil or Michael Kors, a Seiko 5 or Citizen Eco-Drive in the same price bracket gives you most of the same visual simplicity with a movement that’s worth servicing. The Tissot Quartz lineup, around $200-$300, adds a sapphire crystal and full Swiss service network — two things no Fossil Group watch offers at that price. Tissot and Hamilton match Fossil’s style, yes, but not its longevity or resale posture.

Do fashion watches hold their value at all?

They don’t — and this is probably the starkest honest answer in this whole space. Fashion watches are fashion accessories, priced and depreciated like accessories. There is no meaningful secondary market for a used Michael Kors, Daniel Wellington, or MVMT. Fossil watches are unapologetic fashion watches: they look nice, but they aren’t meant to be long-term investments.

By contrast, the Seiko, Citizen, and Orient models competing in the same price bracket all have active pre-owned markets and are designed to be serviced. Mechanical watches need regular movement — wearing them keeps oils flowing and prevents parts from seizing. Fashion watches use movements that are typically sealed or not cost-effective to service, so they have a natural ceiling on useful life.

If you’re buying for the occasion, the outfit, and the next two or three years, that’s an honest use case. If you want something you’ll own in ten years or pass on to someone else, look elsewhere.

Is there any case where a fashion watch is the right buy?

This one comes up a lot: yes, there genuinely is.

If you’re buying a watch primarily as a wearable accessory — to pair with specific outfits, match a particular aesthetic, or give as a gift to someone who cares deeply about how a watch looks and very little about what’s inside — a fashion watch is a legitimate choice. Daniel Wellington’s minimalist Scandinavian design has won genuine design awards, and the thin-case, clean-dial format is hard to replicate for under $150 from watch brands without stepping into much more serious territory.

Michael Kors is for people who see a watch as part of the outfit, a piece of jewellery that’s both stylish and functional. That’s a coherent position. The issue is when buyers are told they’re getting “affordable luxury” — a framing that implies horological substance that simply isn’t there. Fashion watch brands continue to market themselves as affordable luxury, though few people in the watch community would accept that description.

The honest test: if you’d describe the watch to a friend as “a nice accessory,” a fashion watch is fine. If you’d describe it as “a quality watch,” you’re likely to be disappointed.

What’s the smartest way to approach a first watch purchase if I’m drawn to fashion watch aesthetics?

Start with the look you want, then find it in a watch brand. That’s the approach that gets you the best of both worlds.

If you like the clean minimalism of Daniel Wellington, look at the Orient Bambino (dress watch heritage, automatic movement, ~$130) or the Tissot Everytime quartz. If you like the sports-casual feel of a Fossil Heritage Automatic, the Seiko 5 Sports covers the same ground with a far more credible NH36 movement and a global service network. Where Fossil puts style first, Seiko is for the person who wants the mechanics and history of watchmaking alongside the everyday wearability — a genuine step up if you want a timepiece rather than just an accessory.

FAQs

Are Michael Kors watches good quality? Michael Kors watches are made by the Fossil Group and use basic Japanese quartz movements, typically worth a few dollars wholesale. They are stylish and wearable but are fashion accessories first, not serious timepieces. Build quality is adequate for everyday fashion wear but falls short under heavy use or long-term ownership.

Is Daniel Wellington worth the money? Daniel Wellington watches are marketed as “affordable luxury” but use mass-produced movements with a high mark-up for the branding. Their minimalist design is genuinely attractive, but at the same price you can buy a Seiko or Tissot with a far better movement, sapphire crystal, and meaningful heritage.

Do fashion watches hold their value? No. Fashion watches from brands like Fossil, MVMT, and Michael Kors depreciate sharply and have little to no resale market. Unlike Seiko, Citizen, or Swiss-made watches, they are not designed to be serviced, which limits their useful life significantly.

What is the difference between a fashion watch and a real watch? A horological watch is designed by a specialist watchmaker and prioritises movement quality, case finishing, and long-term serviceability. A fashion watch is designed by a clothing or accessories brand that prioritises looks and on-trend styling, typically using the cheapest available movement and outsourcing assembly.

What should I buy instead of a fashion watch under $300? At $100-$300, Seiko 5 Sports, Citizen Eco-Drive, and Orient Bambino all offer proven automatic or reliable quartz movements, sapphire or Hardlex crystals, and decades of watchmaking heritage — for the same price or less than a fashion watch. Tissot’s entry quartz lineup is also worth considering near the $300 mark.
