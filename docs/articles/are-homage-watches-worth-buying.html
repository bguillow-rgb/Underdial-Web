Are Homage Watches Worth Buying? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published August 21, 2026

Quick Answer

Homage watches can be worth buying if execution quality is high and you're honest with yourself about why you want one. The best examples deliver genuine value under $300. But if you'll always be thinking about the original, saving for the real watch, or a well-priced alternative, is the smarter move.

Every week, someone in a watch forum asks some version of the same question: “This $200 watch looks almost identical to a $900 one — is that wrong? Should I just buy it?” The homage watch debate is one of the most charged in the hobby, mixing ethics, value, and genuine collector opinion into one messy argument. Here’s an honest take, without the tribal posturing.

So what exactly is a homage watch, and how is it different from a fake?

A question we hear often: the line between “inspired by” and “illegal copy” genuinely confuses a lot of buyers, so let’s fix that first.

A fake (counterfeit) watch sets out to imitate an existing watch and pretend to be the real thing. A homage watch does not feature logos suggesting it’s the original — it carries its own branding and is open about what it is. That distinction matters legally and practically: counterfeits are illegal to import or sell in most countries, while homages occupy entirely legitimate commercial territory.

In practice, a homage watch takes the design language of an iconic piece — the bezel shape of a Submariner, the dial layout of a Speedmaster, the integrated bracelet of a Nautilus — and reproduces it under a different name at a drastically lower price. They’re popular among buyers who like a certain luxury design but aren’t willing or able to spend thousands to acquire it.

The quality spectrum is enormous. A $35 Alibaba homage and a $350 Steinhart Ocean One are both technically homages. They have almost nothing else in common.

Are homage watches ethical to buy and wear?

This one splits the watch community roughly in half, and neither camp is entirely wrong.

The case for: design inspiration has driven fashion and industry for centuries. Functional shapes — a unidirectional dive bezel, a radial dial layout — aren’t owned by any one brand. The best homages use an established design language as a starting point, then earn their place through honest execution and real value. When someone buys a Steinhart and tells everyone it’s a Steinhart, there’s no deception involved.

The case against: the design teams at Rolex, Omega, and Patek spent decades and enormous capital building the aesthetic equity that homage brands borrow for free. Some enthusiasts feel that buying a homage suppresses demand for original work, including from legitimate microbrands creating genuinely novel designs. If this matters to you, it’s a reasonable position.

Where most thoughtful buyers land: wearing a homage without pretending it’s something it isn’t is defensible. Buying one specifically so strangers will think it’s a Rolex is a different thing, and most experienced collectors find that impulse fades quickly once you’re deeper in the hobby.

What does “quality” actually look like at the homage price point?

This one comes up a lot: buyers want to know whether a $200-$350 homage can genuinely compete with a $200-$350 original.

The honest answer is: sometimes yes, sometimes spectacularly no. Here’s what separates the credible options from the landfill candidates:

On the better homages, bright BGW9 lume stays legible longer than you’d expect, and case finishing feels more refined than the price implies — at least on the brands enthusiasts consistently recommend. Below that tier, corners get cut in ways that are invisible in product photos but obvious on the wrist.

Sapphire crystal has been making its way down to sub-$300 price points from several brands, so paying for mineral glass at any price above $150 is increasingly hard to justify, homage or original.

Which homage watches actually have a good reputation?

Readers frequently ask: for names, not just principles. Here’s where the community consensus sits in 2026.

Steinhart (Swiss-assembled, ~$350-$500) is the name most frequently cited when collectors want a homage they won’t feel embarrassed about. The Ocean One series references Submariner proportions while using an ETA 2824-based movement and sapphire crystal — real watchmaking at a real price. The brand has enough of its own identity that ownership feels less borrowed than it does with most homages.

San Martin (China, ~$150-$280) represents the quality ceiling of Chinese homage production. Case finishing rivals some Swiss watches costing three times more, and the NH35 movement is well-proven. The trade-off is zero brand prestige — nobody outside the watch hobby will know the name.

Heimdallr (~$100-$200) sits below San Martin in finishing but well above the generic tier. Useful if you want to try a style before committing to something pricier.

Worth noting: none of these hold resale value meaningfully. If you ever want to sell, expect to recover 30%-50% of what you paid at best.

Is a homage ever smarter than buying the original?

Here’s the uncomfortable truth most homage-positive articles skip: for the same money as a quality homage, you can usually buy an original watch with its own design that will give you more long-term satisfaction.

A San Martin dive homage at $220 puts you in direct competition with the Seiko SKX013, the Orient Mako, and the Citizen Promaster — all original designs, all with brand heritage, all with actual resale markets. The Seiko 5 Sports series comes with a 24-jewel automatic movement and 100M water resistance for $200-$350, and carries the Seiko name, service network, and a decade of enthusiast goodwill.

If you want a specific iconic design that has no affordable original equivalent (the Nautilus integrated case, say, or the Speedmaster’s exact dial layout), a quality homage is a legitimate path. But if the driver is simply “I want a dive watch,” buying an original almost always ends with fewer regrets. The how to build a watch collection piece on this site has more on why spending on originals tends to compound better over time.

What about the “try before you buy” argument?

A question we hear often: some buyers frame a homage as a trial run — “I’ll buy this to see if I like the style, then save for the real thing.”

It works in theory. In practice, a lot of buyers find they’re still thinking about the original even while wearing the homage, and that feeling leads them to buy both. You end up spending more than if you’d just saved for the original from the start.

That said, a $150 homage that confirms you hate wearing a 42mm case before you spend $800 on a Seiko Prospex is genuinely useful. The key is being honest with yourself about whether the homage is a trial or a substitute. Those are very different purchases.

How do I spot a quality homage versus a junk one?

Five things to check before you buy any homage watch online:

  1. Movement disclosure — Does the seller name the movement? NH35, Miyota 9015, or ETA 2824 are credible. “Automatic movement” with no further detail is a red flag.
  2. Crystal type — Sapphire at $150+ is now standard in the better homages. Mineral glass at $250 means margins are being cut elsewhere.
  3. Lug-to-lug measurement — Homages frequently copy dial aesthetics but not proportions. Lug-to-lug distance determines how the watch wraps your wrist: a 41mm watch with a 48mm lug-to-lug can feel enormous, while a 42mm with a 46mm lug-to-lug may feel balanced. Always check this number, not just the case diameter.
  4. Community reviews — r/Watches and WatchUSeek threads on specific references surface real owner photos and problems that product listings hide.
  5. Return policy — Any brand confident in its quality offers returns. Brands that don’t are telling you something.

FAQs

What is the difference between a homage watch and a fake watch? A homage watch takes design inspiration from an iconic model but carries its own brand name and makes no attempt to pass itself off as the original. A fake (counterfeit) watch copies the logo, model name, and all markings to deceive buyers. Homage watches are legal; counterfeits are not.

Are homage watches legal to buy and wear? Yes. Homage watches are legal to purchase and wear in virtually every country. They do not reproduce protected trademarks or logos. Counterfeit watches that copy logos are illegal to import or sell in most jurisdictions.

Which brands make the most respected homage watches? Steinhart (Swiss-assembled, Submariner-inspired), San Martin (Chinese, strong finishing), and Heimdallr are frequently cited as producing well-executed homages. All three sit in the $150-$400 range and use credible movements like the NH35 or Miyota 9015.

Will I regret buying a homage watch instead of saving for the real thing? It depends on your motivation. If you genuinely love the design and have no near-term plan to buy the original, a quality homage is a reasonable choice. If the real watch is what you actually want, the homage often becomes a reminder of what you don’t own, and you end up buying both.

Is there a better alternative to a homage watch? Often yes. Many affordable originals — Seiko, Orient, Citizen, Hamilton — offer strong designs in the same price range as a quality homage. Buying an original at the same price usually delivers a more satisfying long-term ownership experience, better resale value, and no nagging feeling that you settled.
