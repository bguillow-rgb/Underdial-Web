Is Mido a Good Watch Brand? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published October 16, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes, Mido is a genuinely good Swiss watch brand. Founded in 1918 and part of the Swatch Group, it runs the same ETA-derived Caliber 80 movement as Tissot and Longines, 80-hour power reserve, sapphire crystal, solid finishing, at prices typically between $625 and $950 new. It's one of the most underrated names in affordable Swiss watchmaking.

Mido doesn’t advertise much. It doesn’t sponsor big events or plaster its name across sports broadcasts. For a brand that has been making Swiss watches since 1918, it operates with unusual quietness, which is probably why so many buyers land on the same question: is Mido actually any good, or is it just filling shelf space next to Tissot?

The short answer is that Mido is genuinely good, arguably the most underrated brand in the Swatch Group’s entire portfolio. But the longer answer matters more if you’re deciding where to put $700 or $800.

Wait — is Mido even a real Swiss brand, or is it just a Swatch label?

A question we hear often:

Mido was founded in Switzerland in 1918 by George Schaeren and is currently an entry-to-mid-level Swatch Group brand, known for value and build quality. So yes, it’s the real thing: over a century old, Swiss Made certified, and manufactured in Le Locle, the same valley as Tissot.

Mido is often overshadowed by its Swatch Group siblings (Omega, Longines, Hamilton, Tissot), but it consistently delivers watches that deserve more recognition. The lack of marketing muscle is actually a feature for the buyer. That money goes into the watch instead of the campaign.

Mido is a sensible entry point into serious mechanical watchmaking, especially for a first automatic. You get modern movement technology, solid case quality, and a brand history of over 100 years without entering price regions that require justification.

What movement does Mido actually put inside its watches?

This is the question that separates Mido from the crowd of Swiss-labeled watches running anonymous movements.

Mido’s automatics use the Caliber 80, derived from the same ETA/Powermatic 80 platform used across the Swatch Group, delivering roughly 80 hours of power reserve compared to the 38-42 hours typical of a standard ETA 2824-2. The brand also fits Nivachron balance springs for improved magnetic resistance, and prices still land between $625 and $1,075.

That’s a meaningful spec advantage. Most automatic watches in this price range, even respected ones, top out at 38-48 hours of power reserve. Eighty hours means if you take your Mido off Friday evening and pick it back up Monday morning, it’s still running. Both Mido and Tissot offer the 80-hour reserve and sapphire crystals in 316L steel cases, but Mido’s case finishing is generally considered the stronger point of difference at overlapping price points.

How does Mido’s quality compare to Tissot and Hamilton at the same price?

Readers frequently ask:

All three sit inside the Swatch Group’s mid-tier alongside Certina. Mido lands in roughly the same band as Hamilton and Certina, but the brands are not identical. Each has a distinct design personality and a different sweet spot on the price curve.

Is Mido worth buying compared to a Japanese alternative like Seiko or Orient Star?

This one comes up a lot, and it’s a genuinely fair fight rather than an obvious call.

At $700-$900, Mido goes up against watches like the Seiko Presage Sharp-Edged series and the Orient Star Contemporary, both of which offer serious in-house Japanese movements and exceptional dial finishing. The Swiss vs. Japanese debate at this price tier is one we cover in depth in our Japanese vs Swiss watch guide, but the quick version: Japanese brands at this price often deliver superior dial artistry and in-house movement bragging rights, while Mido delivers the Swiss Made cachet, a longer manufacturer service network in North America and Europe, and the practical comfort of the 80-hour power reserve in daily wear.

Mido is aiming to be the most honest Swiss watch at a given price, not the most artful or the most technically exotic. For a lot of buyers, that’s exactly the brief.

If “Swiss Made” matters to you for personal or professional reasons, Mido is the most efficient way to get it under $1,000. If dial craftsmanship and in-house movement prestige are the priority, the Japanese tier at this price is hard to argue with. Neither is a wrong answer.

What are Mido’s main watch collections, and which is right for me?

Mido’s most popular collections are the Ocean Star, Multifort, Commander, and Baroncelli. Each suits a different buyer:

  • Ocean Star 200 / 600 — The dive line. The Ocean Star 200 (~$750-$850) is the most-recommended starting point: 41mm, 200m water resistance, Caliber 80, sapphire crystal, solid bracelet. The Ocean Star 600 pushes to 600m and a chunkier case for serious divers.
  • Multifort — The field and sport line. Bolder case shapes, some chronograph options, and the TV Big Date variant that leans into vintage styling. The Multifort 8 Two Crowns is a modern steel sports watch with an integrated bracelet that borrows design cues from the Audemars Piguet Royal Oak at a far more approachable price.
  • Baroncelli — The dress line. Slim profiles, leather straps, Roman or index dials. The go-to if you want something that fits under a shirt cuff and reads as understated Swiss without being flashy.
  • Commander — The vintage-inspired line, ranging from around $300 to $1,000, with cushion-case heritage dating back to the 1950s. A quieter choice that rewards people who do their homework.

If you’re building a first watch collection, the Ocean Star 200 covers sport and everyday wear while the Baroncelli handles formal occasions. That pairing spans most situations for under $1,800 combined.

Do Mido watches hold their value, and what should I expect on the secondary market?

This one comes up a lot:

Mido doesn’t hold value the way a Tudor Black Bay or an entry Omega does. That’s an honest reality. But it holds value considerably better than most fashion-adjacent Swiss brands at similar retail prices, and the drop after purchase is moderate, especially for the Ocean Star and Multifort lines.

In practical terms: buy an Ocean Star 200 at $800 new, sell it two years later in solid condition, and you’d realistically recover $430-$550 depending on reference and market timing. That’s a better retention rate than a comparably priced fashion house’s automatic and roughly in line with Hamilton’s secondary market performance. For a watch you’re buying to wear rather than to flip, that’s a reasonable outcome.

Where Mido has a real advantage over some competitors is parts availability and service infrastructure. As a Swatch Group member, authorized service centers exist in most major markets, and ETA-derived movements are among the easiest in the world for independent watchmakers to service. Is Mido a good first “serious” watch?

For the combination of an 80-hour power reserve, sapphire crystal, and genuine Swiss assembly at $625-$1,075, Mido is hard to beat. The brand won’t carry the resale prestige of a Tudor or an Omega, but it also isn’t priced as if it should. You’re paying for engineering and design, not brand markup.

That’s a useful way to think about it. Mido rewards the buyer who has done enough research to know what they’re looking at: who understands that the Caliber 80 is meaningfully better than a base ETA 2824, and that 80 hours of reserve is a practical daily benefit rather than just a spec sheet number. FAQs

Is Mido a luxury watch brand? Not in the traditional sense. Mido sits in the accessible-Swiss or “affordable luxury” tier rather than the high-luxury tier occupied by Omega or Rolex. It is Swiss Made, mechanically legitimate, and built to the same quality standards as its Swatch Group siblings, but priced to compete with mid-range and Japanese watchmakers rather than with true luxury houses.

What movement does Mido use? Most current Mido automatics run the Caliber 80, an ETA-derived movement with roughly 80 hours of power reserve and a Nivachron balance spring for improved magnetic resistance. It is the same platform family that supplies Tissot and Longines.

How does Mido compare to Tissot? Both brands are Swiss Made Swatch Group members running 80-hour automatic movements in sapphire-crystalled 316L steel cases. Mido tends to offer more case-finishing detail above $800; Tissot offers a wider entry range and stronger brand recognition globally.

Do Mido watches hold their value? Moderately. The price drop after purchase is moderate, especially for popular lines like the Ocean Star and Multifort. Expect to recover roughly 50-65% of retail on a well-kept reference: better than fashion-tier Swiss brands, but below Tudor or Omega on the resale curve.

What is the best Mido watch to buy under $1,000? The Ocean Star 200 is the most consistent recommendation: 41mm, 200m water resistance, Caliber 80 movement, sapphire crystal, and a well-finished bracelet at around $750-$850 new. The Baroncelli is the dress-watch pick; the Multifort suits those who want something bolder or sport-oriented.
