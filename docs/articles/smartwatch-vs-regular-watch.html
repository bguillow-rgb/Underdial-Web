Smartwatch vs Regular Watch: Which Should You Buy?

By Underdial · Published August 28, 2026

Quick Answer

A smartwatch wins if daily health tracking and notifications are your priority. A regular watch wins if you want something that lasts decades, holds its look, and works without charging. Under $1,000, traditional watches deliver far more craftsmanship per dollar, but the right choice depends on why you're buying.

Most people approach this choice backwards. They ask “which is better?” when the more useful question is “what am I actually buying it for?” A smartwatch and a traditional watch do genuinely different jobs, sit at different price-quality curves, and age in completely different ways. This guide maps out where each type earns its money, so you can spend once and spend right.

”I already check my phone for the time — why would I buy a regular watch at all?”

A question we hear often: and it’s a fair one. The phone-has-a-clock argument has been made since the early 2000s, but it misses what a well-chosen traditional watch actually does.

A traditional watch — whether a Seiko automatic or a Tissot quartz — is a physical object with a purpose that outlasts its battery, its operating system, and its manufacturer’s support window. Mechanical watches under $1,000 offer real craftsmanship, recognizable design, and daily wear value without requiring a luxury-watch budget. The glance-at-wrist habit is also genuinely faster than the phone ritual: digging into your pocket, pulling out your phone, and waking the screen is nowhere near as quick as a wrist check.

There’s also a category difference that matters financially. Under $1,000, the traditional watch market is remarkable. The Swiss brands populating that price range in 2026 represent genuine Swiss-made instruments from manufacturers with decades of history, built to standards that put watches at ten times the price to shame on pure specification. No smartwatch at that price can make a comparable claim about what’s inside.

”But smartwatches do so much more — health tracking, notifications, GPS. Doesn’t that make them worth it?”

Yes, if those features are things you’ll actually use every single day. In 2026, smartwatches track heart rate zones, VO2 max estimates, training load, recovery status, running power, cadence, route mapping with dual-frequency GPS, and swimming metrics including stroke detection. For runners, cyclists, and anyone managing a chronic health condition, that’s genuinely useful. The Apple Watch Series 11 uses its optical heart sensor to analyze blood flow patterns over 30-day windows, flagging signs of chronic hypertension — a feature Apple expanded to more than 150 countries and regions by January 2026.

The catch is longevity. Smartwatches are consumer electronics, and they age like consumer electronics. Software support typically runs four to six years before updates stop, at which point a watch that cost $399 becomes a paperweight. Traditional watches don’t have that problem: a Seiko NH35-powered watch bought today will still run in 2045. It just needs a service every five to seven years costing roughly $100-$200. That’s a very different total-cost-of-ownership calculation.

Depreciation is the other issue. A new Tissot PRX costs $695 retail; the same watch in excellent condition trades for around $650 pre-owned — you avoid the initial depreciation entirely. A smartwatch at the same price loses 50-70% of its market value within two years as newer models replace it. If you care at all about what your purchase is worth in three years, the traditional watch wins by a wide margin.

At $300-$500, a traditional watch buys you a Swiss or Japanese movement, sapphire crystal on many models, and finishing quality that holds up aesthetically for life. The smartwatch at that price buys you a feature set that will feel dated in three years. Neither is wrong — they suit different people.

”Is there any scenario where I should just get both?”

Readers frequently ask: whether splitting the budget makes sense. The honest answer is yes, if your budget allows it and your lifestyle actually demands it.

Many buyers end up wearing a fitness smartwatch during workouts and a traditional watch for everything else — office, social events, travel. The combined cost of a solid fitness tracker (around $200-$300) and a quality entry-level automatic like an Orient Bambino V2 (around $175-$250) sits well under $600 total. That covers everything without forcing a compromise.

If budget is tight and you can only own one watch, the question becomes simple: do you exercise seriously and need health data, or do you want a daily-wear piece that suits every context and improves with time? A good watch should align with your wrist size, your wardrobe, and the occasions you wear it to — a smaller dress watch for formal settings, a sportier case for outdoor use. A smartwatch is the right choice if fitness tracking is genuinely central to your life. A traditional watch is the right choice if versatility, longevity, and style across contexts matter most.

”Which holds up better over time — a smartwatch or a traditional watch?”

This is where the gap between the categories is starkest, and it’s worth being honest about it.

Many shoppers decide too quickly. A good rating, a famous brand name, or a low price doesn’t mean the watch fits your wrist, wardrobe, maintenance expectations, or ownership style. That’s true for both categories, but time cuts very differently between them. A traditional watch that suits you in 2026 will still suit you in 2036. The design doesn’t go stale because it isn’t tied to software, and the movement runs on physics, not firmware. A craftsman can service a Seiko or Hamilton movement decades from now using techniques and parts that remain available industry-wide.

A smartwatch is tied to an ecosystem. Switching phone platforms later can strand the watch entirely — Garmin is the exception, working fully on both platforms, which is why it suits athletes who may switch. When a manufacturer stops supporting a smartwatch OS, the device loses functionality even if the hardware is perfectly fine. That’s a fundamentally different ownership proposition than something you can hand to your kid someday.

For collectors, traditional watches build history. The Seiko SARB033, discontinued in 2018, has become one of the most sought-after affordable watches in the hobby. Its 38mm case with sunburst black dial hits a sweet spot Seiko has never quite replicated, with prices climbing from $300 new to $570+ pre-owned. No smartwatch will ever have that story.

”What should I actually do if I’m undecided right now?”

Write down your three most important reasons for wanting a watch. If two or more of them are health tracking, fitness logging, or notification management — buy the smartwatch. If two or more are style, longevity, craftsmanship, or collecting — buy the traditional watch.

Then, before you pull the trigger, check the traditional watch against your wrist size, your existing collection, and your budget using Underdial. The app does exactly that job — confirming whether a specific watch suits your proportions and doesn’t duplicate something you already own — so you don’t end up with a 45mm case that swamps a 6.5-inch wrist, or a third blue dial when you already have two. It’s the kind of five-minute check that saves real money and real regret.

The watch market under $1,000 in 2026 is genuinely outstanding. Brands like Seiko, Christopher Ward, Tissot, and Baltic are delivering specs and finishes that cost $5,000 from comparable brands in previous decades. Don’t rush that decision — and don’t default to a smartwatch simply because it’s the louder option in the room.

FAQs

Can a smartwatch replace a regular watch? For timekeeping and basic daily use, yes. But a smartwatch can’t replicate the long-term durability, serviceability, or visual appeal of a well-made mechanical or quartz watch. Most enthusiasts end up owning both.

Do smartwatches hold their value? Almost never. Most smartwatches lose 50-70% of their value within two years as new models release. Traditional watches — especially from Seiko, Hamilton, or Tissot — depreciate far more slowly and some appreciate over time.

Is a $400 smartwatch better than a $400 regular watch? Depends what “better” means. A $400 smartwatch buys you health tracking features. A $400 regular watch buys you Swiss or Japanese mechanical craftsmanship, sapphire crystal, and something built to outlast the decade. Neither is wrong — they serve different needs.

What are the downsides of a smartwatch? Daily or every-other-day charging, a lifespan tied to software support (usually 4-6 years before updates stop), and a look that dates quickly. They also don’t pair well with formal or business-dress contexts the way a traditional watch does.

Can I wear a regular watch and a smartwatch? Yes, and many people do — a fitness-focused smartwatch during workouts and a traditional watch the rest of the time. If you only want one watch, clarifying your primary use case (health tracking vs. daily style) makes the decision straightforward.
