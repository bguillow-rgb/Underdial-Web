Is the Orient Kamasu Worth Buying? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published October 23, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes. The Orient Kamasu is worth buying for most people who want a genuine automatic diver under $400. It delivers sapphire crystal, a screw-down crown, 200m water resistance, and Orient's own hacking, hand-winding F6922 movement, a specification package that nothing else in its price bracket matches.

The Orient Kamasu keeps landing near the top of budget-diver shortlists for a simple reason: it offers a specification sheet that costs meaningfully more on comparable watches from other brands. But spec sheets don’t tell you everything, and plenty of buyers still wonder whether the Kamasu is actually worth their money or whether the hype has outrun the reality. Here’s an honest look at both sides.

Is the Orient Kamasu actually a good watch, or just a good spec sheet?

A question we hear often: affordable watches sometimes win on paper and disappoint on the wrist. The Kamasu is not one of those cases.

The Kamasu is built around a 41.88mm stainless steel case with sapphire crystal, 200m water resistance, a 22mm lug width, and a case thickness of 12.8mm. Those numbers translate to a watch that wears comfortably without feeling stubby or excessively thick, which matters more than it sounds for something you’ll put on every morning. The sunburst dials (available in black, blue, green, red, and more) give it genuine visual presence that punches above its street price.

On paper the Kamasu offers more per dollar than almost anything else in its category: sapphire, 200m, an in-house hacking automatic, and a fully solid bracelet at a street price around $235-$375 is a specification no competitor currently matches. Critically, that advantage holds up in daily use. The crystal stays clear through the knocks and scrapes that would leave a mineral-glass rival looking tired within months.

The Kamasu is considered one of the best dive-style watches available at this price, and is often called an absolute spec monster for its price bracket.

What exactly is the F6922 movement, and is it reliable?

The Kamasu is powered by Orient’s in-house Caliber F6922, described as a successor to the original 469-series workhorse. This updated automatic can be hand-wound and features a hacking function that stops the seconds hand for more precise time-setting.

The movement runs 22 jewels at 21,600 beats per hour with roughly 40 hours of power reserve. You can hand-wind it by turning the crown to restart a stopped watch, and it hacks: pull the crown and the seconds hand stops so you can set the time to the second. That combination is not guaranteed on every automatic in this price range.

In real-world accuracy, expect a few seconds of drift per day in either direction. That’s normal for a watch like this. It is not a chronometer, and if you wear it regularly and rest it in a consistent position overnight you can reduce the drift yourself. Compared to a Seiko 4R36, the F6922’s published accuracy spec is slightly tighter, though both calibers perform similarly in casual use.

How does the Orient Kamasu compare to the Orient Mako?

This one comes up a lot. The Mako is the Kamasu’s older sibling and still a legitimate choice, but the differences matter.

The F6922 in the Kamasu hacks, meaning the seconds hand stops when you pull the crown for precise time-setting. This is a genuine functional advantage over the Mako’s older configuration, which does not hack.

The bigger day-to-day difference, though, is the crystal. The Kamasu is essentially the Mako’s sapphire-crystal sibling: same F6922 caliber, same genuine 200m, but with a far more scratch-resistant crystal. Sapphire stays clear for years; mineral collects hairline scratches over time.

At a price premium of approximately $30-$50 over the Mako on most retailers, the Kamasu delivers upgrades directly proportional to its additional cost. The sapphire crystal advantage alone justifies the premium for buyers who plan to wear the watch daily.

How does the Kamasu stack up against Seiko alternatives?

Readers frequently ask whether the Kamasu is genuinely better than a Seiko at the same price, or whether Seiko’s brand strength makes the comparison obvious.

The Seiko 5 Sports offers the widest choice of dial styles, a case size for every wrist, and the easiest automatic watch on earth to get parts and service for. The Orient Kamasu, by contrast, delivers the stronger spec sheet for the money: sapphire crystal, a screw-down crown, and double the water resistance of basic Seiko 5 references. The tradeoff is that the Kamasu lineup is built almost entirely around one dive-watch shape.

Orient’s luminous compound is adequate rather than notable, and this is the clearest area where Seiko’s Prospex line pulls ahead. If you work in low light or do any actual night diving, Seiko’s Super-LumiNova application on the Prospex is meaningfully brighter and longer-lasting.

One practical consideration that tilts some buyers back toward Seiko: Orient’s repair network is considerably smaller, particularly outside major urban markets. If luminous performance is a priority, or if you want a brand with service centres in every major city, the Kamasu may not be your best call. Who should actually buy the Orient Kamasu?

The Kamasu earns a clear recommendation for a few types of buyers.

Daily-wear beater buyers. If you want one watch that can go from a desk to the pool to a weekend hike without anxiety, the Kamasu’s sapphire crystal and 200m rating mean you genuinely don’t need to coddle it. It’s a full-fledged diver with a screw-down crown and a unidirectional bezel. The sapphire crystal stays clear for years, and the F6922 carries the same 200m rating as the more expensive competition. For regular, heavy use, it’s a hard argument to beat.

First-automatic buyers. The F6922’s hand-winding capability means you can learn the feel of a mechanical movement without depending entirely on wrist motion to keep it running, which is a genuine advantage when you’re new to automatics. See our What Watch Should I Buy First guide for broader context on picking a starter piece.

Buyers priced out of the Seiko Prospex. Entry Prospex models like the SRPD series typically run $300-$450 but use mineral crystal. The Kamasu offers sapphire at a lower street price. That’s a hard trade-off to argue with.

What are the real weaknesses to know before buying?

Honest answer: there are two that matter.

The bracelet. Bracelets have never been Orient’s strong point. The stock bracelet can rattle slightly and the end links feel less premium than the case deserves, which is understandable at this price point but still noticeable. The practical fix is easy: a $15-$25 aftermarket rubber or NATO strap from any reputable strap seller transforms the wearing experience, and the 22mm lug width gives you an enormous selection to choose from.

After-sales support. Orient’s authorised service network is considerably smaller than Seiko’s. If something goes wrong outside warranty, finding a qualified watchmaker is your responsibility. For most buyers who simply wind and wear, this is a non-issue for a decade or more given the F6922’s proven reliability, but it’s worth knowing going in.

Is the Orient Kamasu a good gift watch?

A question we hear often, especially around holiday season. The short answer is yes, with one caveat: the Kamasu skews toward someone who already knows they want a mechanical dive watch. The all-business, sport-forward aesthetic isn’t as universally wearable as a dress-forward piece like the Orient Bambino. But for someone who swims, surfs, hikes, or simply wants a rugged daily driver they’ll wear for years, it’s an excellent choice in the $300-$400 gift range.

If you’re buying for someone else and unsure about wrist size, note that the Kamasu’s lug-to-lug measurement is approximately 46.8-47mm, a dimension that some collectors argue matters more than case diameter itself for fit. Frequently asked questions

How much does the Orient Kamasu cost? MSRP sits between $525 and $580 depending on dial and strap, but street prices at authorized dealers regularly land in the $335-$400 range, making the real-world cost considerably lower than the sticker.

What movement is in the Orient Kamasu? The Kamasu runs Orient’s in-house Caliber F6922: a 22-jewel, 21,600 vph automatic with both hand-winding and a hacking seconds function. It has approximately 40 hours of power reserve.

Is the Orient Kamasu better than the Orient Mako? On paper, yes. The Kamasu adds a sapphire crystal (vs. mineral on the Mako) and retains the F6922’s hacking function. For daily wearers who will knock the watch around, the sapphire alone justifies the modest price premium.

Can you actually dive with the Orient Kamasu? The Kamasu carries a genuine 200m water resistance rating with a screw-down crown and unidirectional bezel, so it meets the functional requirements for recreational diving, though ISO dive-watch certification is not stated by Orient.

What are the Orient Kamasu’s biggest weaknesses? Two recurring criticisms: the bracelet uses hollow end links, which feel less premium than the case deserves, and Orient’s after-sales service network is thinner than Seiko’s, so finding a local watchmaker who knows the movement takes more effort.
