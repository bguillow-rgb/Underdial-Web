Is Tissot a Good Watch Brand? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published October 6, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes. Tissot is a genuine Swiss watchmaker founded in 1853 and part of the Swatch Group. It sits at the entry-level of Swiss luxury, above fashion watches, below Longines, and delivers sapphire crystals, solid automatic movements, and Swiss Made credentials for $350, $900. A strong choice for most buyers under $1,000.

Tissot is one of the first names that comes up when someone spends serious time researching their first Swiss watch, and for good reason. The brand threads a needle that very few others manage: genuine Swiss credentials, a long manufacturing history, and prices that stay within reach of most budgets. But “good” depends on what you’re comparing it to and what you actually want. Here’s the honest answer.

So, is Tissot actually a good brand — or is it just famous?

Tissot was founded in 1853 by Charles Felicien Tissot and his son Charles-Emile Tissot in Le Locle, Switzerland, and is among the oldest of current watchmakers. That’s not marketing fluff — over 170 years of uninterrupted Swiss production is a real thing, and Tissot remains among the most popular accessible brands today.

The more useful frame is where Tissot sits in the hierarchy. It’s a genuine Swiss watchmaker with 170 years of continuous production, placed in the entry-level luxury segment: above fashion watches and most Japanese alternatives, below Longines and TAG Heuer. That position is a feature, not a flaw. You get genuine Swiss Made quality, sapphire crystals, and sophisticated movements at prices that undercut what brands in the tier above charge for equivalent specifications.

The short answer: yes, Tissot is a genuinely good brand, and one of the better-value options in the entire sub-$1,000 watch market.

Who owns Tissot, and does that matter?

A question we hear often: buyers want to know whether the Swatch Group connection is a good thing or a cause for concern.

Tissot joined the Swatch Group in 1983. The Swatch Group is the world’s largest watch manufacturer, and membership gives Tissot access to ETA movements, shared R&D, and a global distribution and service network that independent brands at this price simply cannot match.

Tissot is comparable to Hamilton, Rado, and Mido — all mid-range Swatch Group brands with wide global availability. If you’ve been comparing Tissot with Hamilton (which sits at a similar tier within the same conglomerate), you’re essentially comparing siblings. Both draw on the same movement family; the differences come down to design language and brand heritage. We cover that in detail in our Hamilton vs Tissot comparison.

The Swatch Group connection is, on balance, a positive: it means parts availability, a global service infrastructure, and movement quality that a small independent brand can rarely promise at these prices.

What does a Tissot actually cost in 2026?

This one comes up a lot. Tissot’s pricing range is wider than many people expect.

Tissot pricing spans roughly $150 to $3,000 across the current lineup, with the brand averaging around $400 overall. Quartz basics like the Everytime and entry T-Classic pieces sit under $400. The PRX runs $375-$425 in quartz and roughly $650-$900 in Powermatic 80 automatic.

What’s the movement situation — are Tissot movements any good?

Readers frequently ask whether Tissot movements are in-house or outsourced, and whether that should worry them.

Most of Tissot’s mechanical catalog runs on ETA-based movements, primarily the Powermatic 80 family. These are Swiss-made calibers from ETA, a reputable movement manufacturer, and the 80-hour power reserve is the headline spec that matters. The traditional industry standard sits at 38-42 hours. Tissot’s Powermatic 80 more than doubles that. In practical terms, you can take it off on Friday evening and it will still be running on Monday morning. For a daily-wear automatic, that removes almost all the frustration of resetting a stopped watch at the start of the week.

The movements are not manufactured in-house the way Seiko builds its own calibers from scratch — but that’s the wrong benchmark at this price. ETA-based movements are among the most reliable, most serviceable, and most widely understood calibers in the world. Every watchmaker on earth can work on them. That’s a genuine long-term ownership advantage.

Which Tissot collection should I actually consider?

This is the question that matters most, and it depends heavily on what you’ll use the watch for.

PRX Powermatic 80 (~$700-$850): If you want one Tissot that does almost everything, this is the pick. The integrated bracelet and the 80-hour movement are very hard to beat at this price. The 1970s-inspired design sits comfortably between sport and dress, works on a bracelet or leather strap, and has earned a genuine following in the watch community.

Seastar 1000 Powermatic 80 (~$625-$880): This delivers 300-metre water resistance, a unidirectional rotating bezel, screw-down crown, and the ETA C07.111 movement — the same 80-hour caliber inside the PRX. If you want a proper tool diver rather than a sport-dress hybrid, the Seastar is a compelling answer. It goes up against the Seiko Prospex line at similar prices, a comparison we break down in our Seiko Prospex vs Citizen Promaster guide.

Le Locle Powermatic 80 (~$475-$600): The aesthetic here is classic: guilloche dial patterns, Roman or Arabic numerals, a smooth leather strap that suits suit-and-tie occasions well. It’s not built for adventure (50-metre water resistance only), but as a workhorse dress watch with Swiss automatic movement and sapphire crystal under $500, nothing beats it at this price.

If you’re comparing the PRX against other integrated-bracelet watches like the Citizen Tsuyosa, we cover that head-to-head in our Tissot PRX vs Citizen Tsuyosa article.

What are Tissot’s weaknesses — what does it get wrong?

A question we hear often: most Tissot reviews lean positive, so it’s worth being honest about the limitations.

The main one is movement exclusivity. Unlike Seiko or Orient, Tissot does not manufacture its own calibers from scratch. The Powermatic 80 is an ETA derivation — excellent and reliable, but not proprietary. If movement provenance matters deeply to you, that’s a real point of difference versus Japanese competitors.

Second: Tissot watches generally do not increase significantly in value. Buy one because you want to wear it, not because you expect it to appreciate. That said, popular references do hold reasonable secondary market value. The PRX Powermatic 80 trades on the secondary market around $397, not far off its retail starting price — reasonable retention for a mid-tier Swiss brand.

Third, some of the quartz and lower-end T-Classic pieces feel underdistinguished for the money. The catalog is wide, and not every model represents the same value. Stick to the automatic lineup — PRX, Seastar, Le Locle, Gentleman — and you’re buying something with genuine mechanical substance.

The bottom line: who should buy a Tissot?

Tissot earns its reputation. For a lot of collectors, a Tissot is their first Swiss mechanical watch, and it tends to hold up well under that scrutiny.

If you want genuine Swiss manufacture, a reliable 80-hour automatic movement, sapphire crystal, and a watch that will draw no skeptical looks at work or at a dinner table, Tissot delivers all of that for under $900 — often well under. The PRX Powermatic 80 in particular is one of the most complete packages in the entire sub-$1,000 market.

If you’re building a collection and already own a Japanese automatic, a Tissot adds a genuinely different character: Swiss provenance, a different design vocabulary, and a movement that any watchmaker in the world can service. Tools like Underdial can help you quickly sense-check whether a specific Tissot model overlaps with what you already own or fits your wrist proportions before you commit.

Tissot isn’t flawless — it doesn’t make its own movements, and it won’t appreciate in value. But “good” rarely means perfect. For what most buyers under $1,000 actually need, Tissot is one of the safest, most satisfying answers in the category.

FAQs

Is Tissot considered a luxury watch brand? Tissot is classified as entry-level Swiss luxury — it carries the legally protected “Swiss Made” label and over 170 years of heritage, but it sits below mid-luxury brands like Longines and TAG Heuer in terms of price and prestige. Think of it as the top of the accessible tier, not the bottom of the luxury one.

Which Tissot model should I buy first? For most buyers, the PRX Powermatic 80 (around $700-$850) is the single strongest recommendation: integrated bracelet design, 80-hour ETA-based automatic movement, sapphire crystal, and 100m water resistance. If your budget is tighter, the Le Locle Powermatic 80 (around $475-$600) delivers the same automatic movement in a classic dress-watch package.

Do Tissot watches hold their value? Most Tissot watches lose some value after purchase — they’re not investment pieces the way certain Rolex models are. However, popular references like the PRX and Seastar 1000 retain value reasonably well compared to fashion watches, and they rarely become worthless on the secondary market.

Is Tissot better than Seiko? They’re close in the $300-$700 range but different in character. Tissot gives you Swiss Made credentials and the excellent Powermatic 80 movement; Seiko counters with its own fully in-house movements, often more distinctive dials, and a stronger modding community. Neither is objectively better — it depends on whether Swiss provenance or Japanese engineering resonates more with you.

Are Tissot watches good quality? Yes. Most Tissot automatics use ETA-based Powermatic 80 movements with an 80-hour power reserve — well above the 38-42 hour industry standard. Cases are stainless steel, glass is sapphire crystal across most of the automatic lineup, and build tolerances are tightly controlled. Quality comfortably exceeds what the price would suggest.

What is Tissot’s most popular watch in 2026? The Tissot PRX is Tissot’s most-discussed model in 2026, particularly the Powermatic 80 automatic version. Its 1970s-inspired integrated bracelet, 80-hour movement, and sub-$900 price point make it one of the most recommended watches at any price level.
