Is a Mechanical Watch Worth It? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published October 20, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes, a mechanical watch is worth it if you value craftsmanship, longevity, and the ritual of wearing a hand-built machine. It's not worth it if you need pin-sharp accuracy or zero maintenance. Under $1,000, strong mechanical options exist from Orient, Seiko, and Tissot.

A mechanical watch is worth it for most buyers who go in with clear eyes. It is absolutely not worth it for buyers who expect it to outperform a quartz on accuracy or maintenance convenience. That’s the honest headline. Under $1,000, brands like Orient, Seiko, and Tissot give you access to genuine mechanical movements at prices that would have been unthinkable two decades ago, and for the right buyer, wearing a miniature hand-built machine justifies every penny. For the wrong buyer, it’s a source of mild irritation every time the watch drifts four minutes and needs resetting.

Wait, what actually makes a watch “mechanical”? I always see that word but I’m not sure I understand it.

A question we hear often:

A mechanical watch runs entirely on a coiled metal spring (the mainspring) rather than a battery. As that spring unwinds, it drives a train of tiny gears, which controls a vibrating balance wheel that parcels out energy in precise intervals. The result is the characteristic sweeping second hand. There are two varieties: manual-wind mechanical, where you physically wind the crown daily or every few days, and automatic mechanical, where a rotor weight spins with your wrist movement and winds the spring for you.

The critical distinction from quartz: no battery, no circuit board, no electronic oscillator. Every timekeeping function happens purely through metal parts interacting with each other. Quartz movements have fewer than ten moving parts compared to the 100-plus components in a mechanical watch. That complexity is simultaneously the reason mechanical watches cost more, fascinate more, and need more periodic attention.

So is a mechanical watch actually accurate? I heard they’re terrible at keeping time.

“Terrible” is too strong, but the gap is real. A quality quartz movement keeps time to within 15 seconds per month, while a quality automatic may gain or lose between 5 and 25 seconds per day. That means your automatic could be three to four minutes off after a month without adjustment.

Does that matter? If you’re timing surgery or navigation, yes. If you’re living an ordinary professional life, not remotely. You glance at your phone more than enough to compensate.

There’s one important nuance: some mechanical movements are certified to a tighter standard. Swiss COSC (Controle Officiel Suisse des Chronometres) certification requires mechanical movements to maintain an average daily accuracy of -4/+6 seconds over a rigorous 15-day testing period. Tissot’s COSC-certified models sit at the upper end of the under-$1,000 bracket and get meaningfully closer to that figure in real-world use. But even then, quartz wins the accuracy category without debate. The question is whether accuracy is what you’re actually buying a mechanical watch for.

What does it actually cost to own a mechanical watch over the long run?

This one comes up a lot:

This is where honest math matters. Quartz costs roughly $10-30 per battery every 1-3 years. Automatic servicing runs $200-800 or more every 5-10 years. Servicing a simple Japanese movement (Seiko’s NH35, Orient’s F6922) typically runs $100-200 at an independent watchmaker. A Swiss movement in a Tissot or Mido will generally cost more.

Here’s the flip side: an automatic watch that’s properly serviced will last generations. A quartz movement, once it fails, is often just replaced entirely. Your automatic can outlive you.

If your goal is an heirloom watch, something to pass down, mechanical is the safer bet. Not because quartz is disposable, but because a mechanical movement is repairable by anyone with the training, indefinitely, without needing a part that stopped being manufactured in 1998.

The practical upshot for a buyer spending $200-$600: budget for one service in roughly seven years, treat that as a known cost of ownership, and the maths still work out favourably compared to cycling through two or three cheaper fashion watches in the same period.

What are the actual reasons to buy a mechanical watch, not the romantic marketing version?

Readers frequently ask:

Here’s a clean list, without the lifestyle poetry.

Legitimate reasons to choose mechanical:

  • Longevity. Properly serviced, a mechanical watch from a reputable brand should last 30-50+ years. That changes the cost-per-wear calculation significantly.
  • Repairability. Any competent watchmaker anywhere in the world can service a standard mechanical calibre. You are not dependent on a brand’s proprietary chip being in production.
  • No battery anxiety. You’ll never be caught with a dead watch mid-event. Better movements include a power reserve indicator and predictable winding behaviour.
  • Tactile engagement. Winding a watch, feeling the rotor, watching the movement through a display caseback. These are small daily pleasures that some people genuinely value and others genuinely don’t. Know which camp you’re in before you buy.
  • Resale community. The pre-owned mechanical watch market is large and active, particularly for Seiko, Orient, and Swiss pieces. A quartz fashion watch has near-zero secondary market interest.

Reasons not to choose mechanical:

  • You need the watch to be accurate to the minute for professional reasons.
  • You work in high-vibration environments (heavy machinery, power tools). Vibration stresses mechanical movements.
  • Your budget is firmly under $100. At that tier, mechanical quality drops sharply and quartz wins outright.

The worst mistake is buying a mechanical watch for the wrong reasons. If you expect mechanical to be more reliable, more accurate, or lower-maintenance than quartz, you’ll be disappointed.

I keep seeing “automatic” and “mechanical” used interchangeably. Are they the same thing?

They’re related but not identical. All automatic watches are mechanical; they use the same spring-and-gear system. The difference is how the spring gets wound. An automatic uses a rotating rotor that winds the mainspring via your wrist movement. A manual-wind mechanical requires you to turn the crown yourself, typically daily.

The “automatic” label simply means there’s a rotor doing the winding work. Most mechanical watches sold under $1,000 today are automatic. Pure manual-wind watches at this price tier are rarer and mostly found in dress watch categories.

How do I know if a mechanical watch is right for me specifically?

A question we hear often:

Answer three questions honestly.

1. Do I wear a watch consistently every day? Automatic movements need wrist time to stay wound. If you rotate between several watches or leave one in a drawer for days, it will stop, and you’ll need to reset the time and date every time you pick it up. That’s a mild inconvenience that some people find charming and others find annoying. Know which camp you’re in.

2. Am I buying this watch to last ten or twenty years, or just for the next few? If the answer is long-term, mechanical’s repairability advantage becomes real and significant. If you expect your tastes to change in three years, that advantage evaporates.

3. Can I genuinely afford the occasional service cost? A $150 Orient is excellent value, but a $150-200 service bill in seven years is a real cost on top of it. If that’s a problem at your current budget, a well-made quartz from Citizen or Casio is the smarter call, full stop.

FAQs

Is a mechanical watch more accurate than a quartz watch? No. A quality quartz watch typically drifts only a few seconds per month, while a well-regulated mechanical watch may gain or lose 5-25 seconds per day. The accuracy gap is real, but for everyday life it rarely matters.

How often does a mechanical watch need servicing? Most manufacturers recommend a full service every 5-10 years. Expect to pay roughly $100-$300 depending on the brand and movement complexity. More for Swiss movements, less for Japanese ones.

Can a mechanical watch last a lifetime? Yes. Because a mechanical movement is made of repairable metal parts, it can theoretically be serviced and kept running indefinitely, unlike a quartz movement whose specialist chip may become unavailable decades from now.

What is the cheapest good mechanical watch worth buying? The Orient Bambino starts around $140 with an in-house automatic movement. The Seiko 5 SRPD line starts around $175 with 100m water resistance. Both are genuine mechanical watches, not fashion-brand imitations.

Is a mechanical watch a good investment under $1,000? Very few watches under $1,000 hold or gain value. Buy a mechanical watch because you want to wear and enjoy it, not as a financial investment.
