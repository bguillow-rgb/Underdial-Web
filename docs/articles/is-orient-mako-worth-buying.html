Is the Orient Mako Worth Buying? An Honest Answer

By Underdial · Published November 25, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes, the Orient Mako is worth buying for most people. For roughly $240, you get a genuine 200m-rated dive watch powered by Orient's own in-house automatic movement, a combination almost unmatched at this price. The main trade-off is a mineral crystal instead of sapphire; if that matters, step up to the Kamasu.

The Orient Mako has been quietly earning its reputation for over two decades, and in 2026 it remains one of the most recommended watches in the entire sub-$300 bracket. If you’re trying to decide whether it deserves a place on your wrist (or in your collection), here’s everything you need to weigh, without the hype.

So, is the Orient Mako actually worth buying?

The short answer is yes, almost unequivocally, if a dive watch fits your style.

For roughly $240, you get a 200-meter dive case, a screw-down crown, and Orient’s own F6922 automatic movement. Orient designs and builds that caliber itself, in a price band where almost every competitor buys its movement from someone else. That matters: Orient controls quality end-to-end, servicing is more straightforward, and the movement has decades of real-world track record behind it.

The main honest caveat is the crystal. Orient’s Hardlex mineral glass resists scratches admirably, but it cannot match sapphire’s hardness. If scratch-proof glass is a priority, you’ll want the Kamasu (the Mako’s sapphire-equipped sibling) or to budget for a crystal swap down the line. For most wearers, Hardlex holds up fine in everyday use. It’s a real trade-off, not a deal-breaker.

What exactly do you get for the money?

A question we hear often: what does “in-house movement” actually mean for a $240 watch, and does it matter?

It matters more than most buyers realize. The F6922 caliber is Orient’s own, not an outsourced Miyota or ETA derivative, and it both hacks (the seconds hand stops when you pull the crown) and hand-winds. That makes setting it straightforward, and accuracy is good for a budget automatic. At this price, that combination is genuinely exceptional: many competitors in the $200-$300 range ship movements that neither hack nor hand-wind.

The Orient Mako II is frequently cited as the best-value dive watch you can buy. When it first gained traction, an automatic movement, 200-meter water resistance, and a 41.5mm case under $150 was an extraordinary proposition. Prices have since risen to around $240 at street price, but the value equation is still intact.

The case itself impresses too. The stainless steel construction feels substantial without tipping into oversized territory, and Orient uses brushed finishing on the case sides with polished center links. It manages to feel considered without being showy.

Who is the Orient Mako actually for?

This one comes up a lot: “I’m not a diver, should I still consider it?”

Absolutely. The Mako wears like a tool watch but reads like a sport watch, the kind of piece you can pair with jeans, shorts, or casual work clothes without it looking out of place. The rotating bezel gives you an elapsed-time function that’s just as handy for parking meters and cooking timers as it is for decompression stops.

The watch occupies an interesting market position: too refined for fashion watch buyers, yet affordable enough for entry-level collectors and enthusiasts who won’t spend Seiko Prospex money. That sweet spot is exactly where most first-time automatic buyers land, which is why the Mako keeps appearing at the top of recommendation threads year after year.

If you’re building a small rotation and want one watch that can genuinely do everything, beach, travel, weekend, casual office, the Mako earns its place. You can read more about building that kind of versatile kit in our how to build a watch rotation guide.

The Kamasu is the natural step-up. If you’ll wear the watch occasionally and treat it with some care, the Mako is more than enough. For a thinner wrist, though, neither is the ideal answer: the 39mm Mako 40 is the better call.

The SKX007 comparison is worth addressing directly. Seiko discontinued the SKX, and it now trades on the secondary market at prices well above the Mako’s new retail. Whether you’re a seasoned collector exploring Japanese alternatives to Seiko or someone just getting into automatic watches, the Mako demands serious consideration on specs per dollar alone.

Does it hold up over time?

Readers frequently ask: “I’ll wear it every day, will it last?”

The honest answer is yes, with realistic expectations. In real-world usage across saltwater environments, freshwater lakes, and general daily wear, the Mako has a solid track record for water resistance integrity. Like any automatic, it benefits from periodic servicing. Roughly every five to seven years under normal use is a reasonable expectation, which is consistent with what we cover in our how often to service a watch guide.

The bracelet is frequently mentioned as the weakest point, and that hasn’t changed with newer models. Swapping to a rubber dive strap or a nylon NATO costs $15-$30 and immediately improves comfort and security during aquatic use. A small outlay that completes the package.

Are there cheaper alternatives worth considering instead?

A question we hear often: “Is there anything meaningfully similar for less money?”

Genuinely? Not really, at the in-house automatic level. The Vostok Amphibia gets close in novelty and water resistance, but its movement doesn’t hack or hand-wind in most configurations. The Casio MDV line offers excellent quartz dive styling for under $60, but that’s a different conversation entirely. Quartz vs. automatic is a lifestyle choice more than a spec comparison, and we unpack that in our automatic vs quartz watch under $1,000 guide.

The Orient Mako II is arguably the best entry point into automatic dive watch ownership. That’s not marketing language; it’s a conclusion that watch reviewers, forum regulars, and buyers keep arriving at independently.

If your budget can stretch to around $330 and you’ll wear the watch hard daily, the Kamasu’s sapphire crystal is worth the upgrade. Full details in our is the Orient Kamasu worth buying article. The bottom line

The Orient Mako is worth buying because it doesn’t ask you to compromise on what actually matters in a dive watch: movement quality, water resistance, and case construction. The mineral crystal is a genuine trade-off, not a hidden flaw. Orient clearly understood that buyers at this price point care about usability in real conditions, and the Mako delivers exactly that.

For around $240, it’s one of the few watches in this price bracket that earns a confident “yes” without a string of asterisks attached.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Orient Mako and the Orient Kamasu? Both share Orient’s in-house F6922 automatic movement and 200m water resistance. The Kamasu (sometimes called Mako III) adds a sapphire crystal and usually better lume for around $80-$90 more. The Mako uses Orient’s Hardlex mineral crystal. For hard daily wear, the Kamasu’s sapphire is worth the premium; for occasional wear, the Mako is plenty.

Does the Orient Mako movement hack and hand-wind? Yes. The F6922 caliber hacks, meaning the seconds hand stops when you pull the crown, and it hand-winds, so you don’t have to shake the watch to set it up. Both features are genuinely rare at this price point.

Is the Orient Mako good for actual diving? Yes, it’s a legitimate tool watch. The 200m-rated case features a screw-down crown and a unidirectional elapsed-time bezel. It’s suitable for swimming, snorkeling, and recreational free-diving. The case construction far exceeds what most wearers will ever put it through.

Which Orient Mako size should I choose? The standard Mako II/III sits at around 41-42mm with a lug-to-lug of roughly 46mm, comfortable on medium to larger wrists. If you have a thinner wrist (under about 6.5 inches), the 39mm Mako 40 is a better fit. Check your lug-to-lug tolerance before buying.

How does the Orient Mako compare to the Seiko SKX007? They’re close cousins in DNA. The SKX007 is discontinued and now commands inflated secondary-market prices of $300-$450+. The Mako offers a comparable in-house automatic, identical 200m rating, and screw-down crown at a lower new price. The SKX has stronger brand cachet; the Mako delivers equal or better specs per dollar in 2026.
