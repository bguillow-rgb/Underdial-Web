A GMT watch does one thing no standard watch can: it shows two time zones at the same time without any math. That sounds simple, but the mechanics and the buying decision are more nuanced than most explainers admit. This guide covers exactly how it works, whether your life actually calls for one, and what to realistically expect under $500.

## What does a GMT watch actually do?

*A question we hear often:* people see a fourth hand on a dial and assume it must be complicated to use. It isn't, once you understand what it's tracking.

A GMT watch adds a fourth hand to the standard hour, minute, and seconds arrangement. That hand completes one rotation every 24 hours rather than 12, and is read against a 24-hour bezel or a chapter ring printed on the dial. You set that hand to a reference time zone, historically Greenwich Mean Time and now whatever second zone you care about, and your standard hands show local time. Two time zones, one dial, zero arithmetic.

A GMT tracks two time zones: local time via the standard hour and minute hands, and a second time zone via a 24-hour hand read against a bezel or dial markings. The 24-hour scale also solves a subtle problem: it immediately tells you whether your reference zone is in AM or PM, which a 12-hour hand cannot.

Originally designed for pilots in the 1950s, the GMT-Master from Rolex helped travelers keep track of time across multiple places. The complication has since filtered down from five-figure Swiss sports watches to reliable sub-$500 Japanese automatics, a shift that happened faster than most people expected.

## What's the difference between a "true GMT" and a dual-time watch?

This is the spec distinction that catches most buyers off guard, and it matters more than case size or bracelet finishing.

The real substance of a GMT watch is in its movement. Understanding that distinction before looking at specific picks will save you a regretful return.

A **true GMT** (sometimes called a "traveler GMT") lets you set the local hour hand independently without stopping the movement. When you land in Tokyo, you advance the local hand to Tokyo time while the 24-hour GMT hand keeps ticking at your home zone. No stopping, no resetting seconds, no disruption. This is what the Rolex GMT-Master II does, and why its Cal. 3285 commands a premium. At the affordable end, the Seiko 4R34 and NH34 movements replicate this behavior.

A **dual-time** (or "office GMT," sometimes marketed as a GMT despite not being one) only reads a second time zone. It does not let you independently adjust the local hour hand. To change local time you wind the crown and advance all hands together. This is fine if you never actually cross time zones and just want to monitor, say, your New York office from London. It becomes a frustration the moment you land somewhere and need to reset quickly.

The practical upshot: if you travel across multiple time zones more than a few times per year, pay close attention to which type you're buying. If you mostly sit at a desk tracking a remote team's time zone, either type works fine.

## Who actually needs a GMT watch?

*This one comes up a lot:* "My phone tracks six time zones. Why would I spend $400 on a watch that tracks two?"

Fair question. When your phone updates to local time automatically and handles multiple zones without any effort, it's worth being honest about whether you really need one.

Here's a straight framework.

**You'll genuinely use it if you:**
- Travel internationally more than four or five times a year and want your home time at a wrist-glance during meetings
- Work remotely with colleagues in a specific time zone you reference constantly
- Are a pilot, flight attendant, or maritime worker (the original use case still holds)
- Find the ritual of wearing and reading it meaningful. For many travelers and enthusiasts, the GMT adds a small ceremony to arriving somewhere, a way of staying connected to where you came from while you settle into somewhere new.

**You probably don't need it if you:**
- Travel domestically or rarely cross more than one time zone
- Already own a [watch with a world-time complication](https://underdial.com/watch-complications-explained)
- Would primarily use it as a style piece, in which case a cleaner dial without the extra hand and bezel may serve you better

## How do you set and read a GMT watch?

*Readers frequently ask:* the setting procedure trips people up more than the reading does, especially on a first GMT.

**Reading it:** The 24-hour GMT hand points to a number (1-24) on the bezel or dial ring. Numbers 1-12 indicate AM; 13-24 indicate PM. Your regular hour and minute hands show local time. Glance at the GMT hand, check the bezel, done.

**Setting it (true GMT):** Pull the crown to the GMT position (usually position 2 on a Seiko, position 3 on some Swiss movements). Rotate the bezel to align your reference time zone with the GMT hand. Push the crown back. Local time continues uninterrupted.

**Setting it (dual-time):** Pull the crown fully and advance the hands until local time is correct. The 24-hour hand moves with the hour hand and must be set in the same step.

One practical note: a rotating bezel (as on the Seiko SSK or Pepsi-style bezels) lets you track a third time zone by rotating the bezel ring manually, though this is a trick rather than a mechanical complication.

## Does a GMT watch cost more to service?

*A question we hear often:* the extra hand and jumping mechanism do add one layer of complexity over a standard three-hand movement.

In practice, expect a service interval similar to any automatic, roughly every 5-7 years under normal use. The additional cost over a standard service is modest. The fourth hand and jumping hour mechanism require extra components and precise calibration, which adds some labor. Independent watchmakers familiar with Seiko or ETA-architecture movements typically charge $50-$100 more than for a comparable non-GMT movement, putting a full service in the $200-$350 range for most affordable automatics. Quartz GMT movements like the Citizen Eco-Drive GMT are simpler and cheaper to service, and their battery-free solar operation means you may go a decade without any service need at all.

## Is a quartz GMT or automatic GMT better under $500?

This is essentially the same question as choosing any automatic vs quartz watch, but the GMT context adds a nuance worth noting.

Match the movement to the job. For frequent travelers who need reliability above everything else, a solar quartz GMT like the Citizen Eco-Drive is nearly maintenance-free and accurate to plus or minus 15 seconds per month. Set it once and forget it. For collectors who enjoy the mechanical side of watchmaking, the Seiko 4R34-powered SSK series delivers a true GMT mechanism with the satisfying sweep of an automatic rotor.

One thing to weigh: automatic GMT movements at this price point run around plus or minus 15-25 seconds per day. If you're relying on it for precision in a professional context, a quartz or solar movement is the more honest tool. ## FAQs

**What does GMT stand for on a watch?**
GMT stands for Greenwich Mean Time, the universal reference the complication was historically calibrated to. The fourth hand was designed to display this reference zone while the main hands showed local time. Today you can set the GMT hand to any second zone you choose. "GMT" is now just the name of the complication.

**What is the difference between a true GMT and a dual-time watch?**
A true (or "traveler") GMT lets you independently advance the local hour hand without stopping the movement, so you can switch time zones mid-trip instantly. A dual-time only lets you *display* a second time zone; resetting requires winding all hands together. The distinction matters most if you physically cross time zones regularly.

**Do I need a GMT watch if I have a smartphone?**
For pure utility, probably not. Your phone handles multiple time zones automatically. But a GMT is genuinely faster for a wrist-glance at your home time during a meeting, and for many buyers the complication is part of what they enjoy about wearing a mechanical watch.

**Can you get a good automatic GMT watch under $500?**
Yes. The Seiko 5 Sports SSK series, Orient Star GMT, and Citizen Eco-Drive GMT all deliver genuine GMT function at or under the $500 mark, and until fairly recently that would have seemed unlikely.

**How do you read a GMT watch?**
Set the 24-hour hand to your reference time zone. Numbers 1-12 on the bezel or ring mean AM; 13-24 mean PM. Your standard hour and minute hands show local time. The key advantage over a regular watch: one glance tells you both the local time *and* whether your reference zone is in the middle of the night, something no standard dial can do.
