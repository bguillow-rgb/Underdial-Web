The Mido Multifort sits in one of the most competitive corners of the watch market: Swiss automatic, under $1,000, honest tool-watch DNA. It quietly holds its own against flashier names that spend more on marketing than on movement finishing. If you've been circling it and want a straight answer before you spend, here it is.

## So, is the Mido Multifort actually worth the money?

**Short answer: yes, for most buyers in this price range.** The Multifort's core proposition is straightforward. A Swiss automatic movement with an 80-hour power reserve, sapphire crystal with anti-reflective coating, and an exhibition caseback, all at a street price that typically lands between $600 and $950 depending on the reference. That is a genuinely strong specification sheet for the money, and Mido's quality control at this tier has a reputation for consistency that few brands of similar size can match.

The longer answer depends on what you're optimizing for. If you want an integrated-bracelet sport-dress look, there are sharper options (the Tissot PRX is the obvious one). If you want a maximally dressy dial, the Orient Bambino or Seiko Presage give you more character for less money. But if you want a Swiss automatic built as a reliable, long-wearing daily watch with a movement that won't strand you mid-week, the Multifort earns its place.

One detail that matters: Mido does not use the version of the Powermatic 80 with the plastic rotor component that Tissot employs, a distinction that enthusiasts notice even if it rarely surfaces in mainstream reviews.

## What exactly is Mido's Caliber 80, and why does the power reserve matter?

*A question we hear often:* buyers coming from Seiko 5 territory sometimes ask whether 80 hours of power reserve is marketing language or something practically useful.

It's practically useful. The standard Seiko NH35 and Miyota 8215 movements both offer roughly 40-42 hours of reserve. The Mido Caliber 80 is an automatic movement based on the ETA 2824-2 platform, tuned for double the conventional reserve. In practice, 80 hours means you can take the watch off on Friday evening and put it back on Monday morning, roughly 60 hours later, without resetting the time. For anyone who rotates between two or three watches, that change is immediately noticeable.

The Chronometer 1 variant of the Multifort goes further, achieving COSC chronometer certification. Standard automatic movements at this price are typically accurate within roughly ±10 seconds per day, but the COSC-certified Multifort Chronometer is rated within ±4 seconds per day. That's one of the tightest tolerances available under $1,000 without buying a used or grey-market piece. If movement accuracy is a priority, that variant is worth the small premium over the standard reference.

## How does the Multifort compare to the Tissot PRX and Hamilton Khaki Field?

*This one comes up a lot:* the Multifort, the Tissot PRX Powermatic 80, and the Hamilton Khaki Field Auto are the three Swiss automatics most frequently compared in the $500-$950 range. Here's how they actually stack up:

| Spec | Mido Multifort (standard) | Tissot PRX Powermatic 80 | Hamilton Khaki Field Auto |
|---|---|---|---|
| Movement | Caliber 80 (ETA 2824-2 base) | Powermatic 80 | H-10 (ETA 2824 base) |
| Power reserve | 80 hours | 80 hours | 80 hours |
| Crystal | Sapphire, AR-coated | Sapphire, AR-coated | Sapphire |
| Water resistance | 100m (most refs) | 100m | 100m |
| Case style | Traditional/cushion | Integrated bracelet | Field/cushion |
| Typical street price | $600-$950 | $675-$775 | $495-$695 |
| Exhibition caseback | Yes (most refs) | Yes | Yes |
| COSC option | Yes (Chronometer 1) | No | No |

The PRX wins on integrated-bracelet aesthetics: it's the closest thing to a Patek Nautilus silhouette at this price. The Hamilton wins on value and military heritage. The Multifort wins on movement specification depth (COSC option, rotor quality) and wears more neutrally, which makes it easier to pair across styles. If you're building a [watch rotation](https://underdial.com/articles/how-to-build-a-watch-rotation) where the second or third slot needs a versatile Swiss automatic that doesn't scream any single archetype, the Multifort is probably the right answer.

## Does the Mido Multifort hold its value, and should that influence my decision?

*Readers frequently ask:* whether value retention should factor into an under-$1,000 purchase. The honest answer is that it matters less here than people think, but the picture is better than most assume.

The Multifort trades on the secondary market for approximately 70-75% of original retail, a depreciation curve that stabilizes relatively quickly. Unlike fashion watches that plummet immediately, it holds because people buy it for genuine functionality rather than passing trends. That means you're losing roughly $150-$250 on a $750 watch if you sell it in a year. That's a real cost, but it's also the floor: it doesn't typically slide further once it reaches that level.

For context, most watches under $1,000 lose 20-40% of their value after purchase, so the Multifort sits at the better end of that range. The Chronometer 1 and limited-edition IBA ("Inspired By Architecture") variants hold slightly better because they attract collector interest beyond everyday buyers.

If value retention is your primary concern, a pre-owned Multifort bought at grey-market or secondary prices is the sharper play: you skip the steepest depreciation immediately. ## Which Mido Multifort reference should I actually buy?

*A question we hear often:* "Multifort" covers a wider family than many buyers realize, including round sport references, the distinctively shaped TV case, and the integrated-bracelet Multifort 8, so the model question matters as much as the brand question.

The Multifort 8 One Crown houses Mido's Caliber 80 inside a 40mm case with an 80-hour power reserve and Nivachron shock resistance. It's the most direct competitor to the Tissot PRX in Mido's lineup, and at its roughly $1,070 USD price point it's hard to find another integrated-bracelet automatic, let alone one with a movement this good, at a comparable price.

For buyers who want a cleaner, more traditional round case, the standard Multifort M references in 40-42mm are the sweet spot: widely available, stocked by authorized dealers, and priced to leave room for a strap upgrade or two. The Multifort TV 28 Quartz is the strongest entry-point recommendation for buyers prioritizing value and classic proportions over case size or automatic movement. If you want something more unusual, the Multifort TV Big Date earns its place with a case shape that has fifty years of real design history behind it, a genuinely useful Big Date complication, and an 80-hour Caliber 80 that holds its own against watches well above this price point.

## Who should *not* buy the Mido Multifort?

Being honest matters more than making a sale. The Multifort is not the right answer for everyone:

- **If your budget is under $500**, the Orient Mako, Seiko 5, or [Seiko Presage entry models](https://underdial.com/articles/is-seiko-presage-worth-buying) deliver more character per dollar in Japanese automatics.
- **If you want maximum dial drama**, the Seiko Presage Cocktail Time or Sharp Edged series have enamel-inspired dials that the Multifort simply doesn't attempt to match.
- **If the integrated-bracelet look is non-negotiable**, the Tissot PRX is better-proportioned for that specific aesthetic.

## FAQs: Mido Multifort

**What movement does the Mido Multifort use?**
Most Multifort references run Mido's Caliber 80, based on the ETA 2824-2 platform and tuned for an 80-hour power reserve. Some variants use the COSC-certified Caliber 80 Si with a silicon balance spring for improved accuracy and shock resistance.

**How accurate is the Mido Multifort?**
Standard references perform within roughly ±10 seconds per day, typical for a quality Swiss automatic. The COSC-rated Multifort Chronometer 1 is certified to within ±4 seconds per day, one of the best accuracy specifications under $1,000.

**Is the Mido Multifort good value compared to the Tissot PRX?**
They target different aesthetics. Both share the Powermatic 80 movement family, but the Multifort leans toward a tool-watch profile while the PRX chases an integrated-bracelet sport-dress look. Mido's rotor uses a metal rather than plastic component, a small but noted quality distinction. If you want the integrated look, the PRX wins on design; if you want a more traditional Swiss automatic with deeper movement spec, the Multifort holds its own at a comparable or slightly lower price.

**Does the Mido Multifort hold its value?**
It depreciates to roughly 70-75% of retail on the secondary market, which is better than the 60-80% loss common across the under-$1,000 segment. It stabilizes quickly because it's bought for genuine utility.

**What wrist size suits the Mido Multifort?**
Most references measure 40-42mm in diameter with a lug-to-lug of roughly 46-49mm, suiting wrists of approximately 6.5-7.5 inches. The TV-shaped variants wear slightly smaller than their stated dimensions suggest, making them an option for smaller wrists who want an automatic in this family.
