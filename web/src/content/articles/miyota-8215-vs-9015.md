Both the Miyota 8215 and the Miyota 9015 come from the same Japanese company, Citizen's dedicated movement-making division, and both power a huge share of the sub-$500 automatic watch market. But they are not interchangeable, and the difference between them is felt every single day on your wrist. Here's the honest breakdown for buyers, not modders.

---

## What even is Miyota, and why does it keep showing up in watch listings?

Miyota movements power millions of watches worldwide, from affordable entry-level automatics to respected mid-range timepieces. As Citizen's movement manufacturing division, Miyota has built a reputation for reliable, accessible calibers that deliver a genuine automatic watch experience without Swiss price premiums.

That last part matters. Swiss movements like ETA and Sellita come at a significant premium; using one in a watch under $500 often means the case, crystal, and finishing take a hit. Miyota lets brands put more money into the parts you actually see and touch.

Miyota produces over 100 million movements annually from factories in Nagano, Japan, which is why you'll see their calibers in everything from $150 microbrands to $600 dress watches. The 8215 and 9015 are the two calibers that come up most often, and they're more different than their adjacent model numbers suggest.

---

## What's the actual difference between the 8215 and 9015?

*A question we hear often:* buyers see both names in listings and assume it's just a small spec bump. It isn't.

| Spec | Miyota 8215 | Miyota 9015 |
|---|---|---|
| Beat rate | 21,600 vph (3 Hz) | 28,800 vph (4 Hz) |
| Thickness | 5.67 mm | 3.9 mm |
| Hacking (stop-seconds) | No | Yes |
| Hand-winding | No | Yes |
| Jewels | 21 | 24 |
| Accuracy (rated) | -20 to +40 s/day | -10 to +30 s/day |
| Power reserve | ~40 hours | ~42 hours |
| Wholesale cost tier | Budget (~$15-25) | Premium (~3x the 8215) |

The gap most buyers notice first is the **sweep of the seconds hand**. The 9015's 28,800 vph beat rate sweeps visibly smoother than the 8215's 21,600 vph. On the 8215, you can see and sometimes hear the individual ticks. The 9015 moves more like a Swiss ETA: fluid, composed, easy to confuse for something twice the price.

The second gap is **case thickness**. At 3.9 mm, the 9015 is one of the thinnest mass-produced automatic movements on the market. That matters a lot for watch design, since it lets brands build slim cases that actually fit under a shirt cuff. The 8215 at 5.67 mm pushes total case thickness past 11 mm almost regardless of how thin the brand tries to go with the case design.

The third gap is **usability**. The 9015 hacks: the seconds hand stops when you pull the crown, letting you set the time to the exact second. It also accepts manual winding, so you can power it up after it's been sitting in a drawer without shaking it for ten minutes. The 8215 offers neither.

## Is the 8215 actually bad, or is it just older?

*This one comes up a lot:* people read "no hacking, no hand-winding" and assume the 8215 is a movement to avoid. That's too harsh.

The Miyota 8200 series, especially the 8215, built its reputation on simplicity and endurance. The 9000 series, led by the 9015, came later and redefined Japanese precision by challenging Swiss standards at a fraction of the cost. The 8215 wasn't replaced; it was supplemented. They occupy genuinely different market positions.

Decades of production and millions of units deployed mean the 8215 is thoroughly vetted. Watchmakers worldwide can service it, failure rates are extremely low, and standard service costs run around $30-60. At a wholesale price of $15-25, the 8215 is also what makes automatic movements possible in watches under $200, which Swiss alternatives simply can't match.

The rotor noise is worth flagging honestly. The 9015 and NH35 are quiet in operation; the 8215 is more audible, though it isn't bothersome in everyday use. If you're coming from a quartz background, hearing the rotor spin when you move your wrist can feel unexpected. It's not a defect. It's just the character of the movement.

## Which one is better for dress watches?

*Readers frequently ask:* whether the movement matters for how a watch looks on the wrist with a suit or at a formal dinner.

It does, indirectly, through thickness. The 9015's slim profile makes it well suited for dress watches, and the movement finishing is noticeably higher quality than the 8-series Miyota calibers. A dress watch powered by an 8215 will almost always sit thicker on the wrist, which is rarely what you want when the goal is something elegant that slides under a French cuff.

If you're shopping for a dress automatic in the $300-$600 range, something slim and dial-forward you'd reach for at dinner, look for the 9015. Many of the best-reviewed microbrands in that bracket specify it for exactly this reason. If you're buying a field or sport watch where a millimeter or two of extra thickness is irrelevant, and the price is under $250, the 8215 is a perfectly honest choice.

## How accurate is the 9015 in real-world use?

Miyota officially rates the 9015 at -10 to +30 seconds per day, which sounds wide. In practice, most properly regulated 9015 movements run within +5 to +10 seconds per day, and well-regulated examples often hit chronometer-grade performance (-4 to +6 s/day) without any official certification. That's more than enough for daily wear. You'll set your watch maybe once a month, not once a week.

The 8215 carries a rated accuracy of -20 to +40 seconds per day, which is meaningfully wider. Real-world performance often beats the factory spec, and many owners get single-digit deviations after proper regulation, but that does require a watchmaker visit, which is an added cost and step the 9015 rarely needs out of the box.

For context: a COSC-certified Swiss movement is rated to ±4 seconds per day. The 9015's 28,800 vph beat rate puts it in the same performance class as the ETA 2824-2 and Sellita SW200, both of which cost significantly more wholesale and appear in watches well above $500.

## At what price point does it make sense to pay for the 9015?

This is the practical question that decides most purchases.

Watches in the $300-$500 range typically justify the 9015. Below that threshold, say a $150-$230 microbrand or entry-level automatic, the 8215 is entirely appropriate and you'd rather the brand spend the saved movement cost on a better case, crystal, or strap. In 2026, the best Miyota-powered watches represent some of the strongest value in the $150-$500 range, and the brands building with them are among the most interesting independents in the hobby.

A rough rule: if the watch costs under $250, don't penalize it for running an 8215. If it costs $350 or more and is marketing itself as a refined automatic, check whether it uses the 9015 or a comparable higher-spec caliber, because at that price the better movement should be there.

## Is there anything between the 8215 and the 9015 worth knowing about?

Yes: the **Miyota 8315**, which sits squarely between the two.

The 8315 adds roughly a 60-hour power reserve and hacking while staying in the 8-series family. It won't sweep as smoothly (still at 21,600 vph), and the case will still be thicker, but for a sport or field watch in the $200-$320 bracket it's a genuinely smart middle ground. You get the two features most people miss on the 8215, hacking and the longer reserve, without fully stepping up to 9015 pricing.

So the short version: the 8215 is the budget workhorse with broad parts support and simple serviceability. The 8315 adds hacking and reserve. The 9015 sweeps smoother and builds into slimmer cases at the higher price tier.

## FAQs

**Is the Miyota 8215 a good movement?**
Yes. The 8215 is a proven, highly reliable movement used in tens of millions of watches. Its main limits are a louder rotor, no hacking or hand-winding, and a wider accuracy tolerance (-20 to +40 s/day). For everyday affordable automatics under $250, it's entirely adequate.

**How accurate is the Miyota 9015 in real life?**
Miyota rates the 9015 at -10 to +30 seconds per day, but well-regulated examples typically run within +5 to +10 s/day. Carefully regulated units can approach chronometer-grade performance (-4 to +6 s/day) without official certification.

**Does the Miyota 8215 have hacking?**
No. The 8215 lacks a stop-seconds (hacking) function and cannot be manually wound. The 9015 includes both, which let you set the time precisely and restart a stopped watch without shaking it.

**Can I hear the rotor on a Miyota 8215?**
More than you might expect. The 8215's rotor is noticeably louder than the 9015's. It's not a defect, it's characteristic of the movement's design, but if a quiet, refined feel matters, the 9015 is the better choice.

**Which microbrand watches use the Miyota 9015?**
Well-known microbrand and independent brands that specify the 9015 include Lorier, Undone, Gruppo Gamma, and Islander. The 8215 appears in a wider range of entry-level automatics from fashion and starter brands.
