The Tissot Seastar 1000 Powermatic 80 keeps coming up in "best Swiss diver under $1,000" conversations, and for good reason. But "shows up in every roundup" and "worth buying for your collection" are two different questions. This article gives you the full picture: what the Seastar actually delivers, where it falls short, who it's right for, and what to consider instead.

---

## What exactly is the Tissot Seastar, and why does everyone keep recommending it?

The Seastar is Tissot's flagship dive-watch line, sitting alongside the PRX and the Gentleman as one of three pillars in the brand's current automatic lineup. It's a genuine dive watch, Swiss-made, and priced where most competitors cut corners.

It's been in production in various forms since 1958, but the modern Seastar 1000, specifically the Powermatic 80 variant, is what most people are asking about in 2026. At its current price, you get 300-metre water resistance, a unidirectional rotating bezel, screw-down crown, and the ETA C07.111 movement, the same 80-hour calibre found inside the PRX.

That movement is the real hook. Most automatic divers at this price run movements with 38-42 hours of power reserve. The Powermatic 80 nearly doubles that, which matters if you take a watch off on Friday evening and want it still running Monday morning.

The Powermatic 80.111 is a 23-jewel movement with hacking seconds, date, Nivachron balance spring, and a frequency of 21,600 bph. The Nivachron spring, an anti-magnetic alloy developed within the Swatch Group, is a meaningful detail at this price: the movement resists everyday magnetic fields from phone chargers, laptop bags, and bag closures without needing a soft-iron cage.

Accuracy runs about -1 to +5 seconds per day, which is respectable for an entry-level Swiss automatic. The official tolerance is wider, but in practice the movement frequently beats its own spec.

## Is the 40mm or the 43mm the right one to buy?

*This one comes up a lot.* The 43mm is the original "hero" reference, bolder, more dive-watch aggressive, and what most review photos show. The 40mm came later as a direct response to buyers who found 43mm impractical for daily wear.

At 40mm in diameter, 12.6mm thick, and 46mm lug-to-lug, the Seastar 1000 hits a sweet spot and wears well as a daily-driver dive watch on a 6.75" wrist.

If your wrist is under 7 inches (around 18cm), the 40mm is almost certainly the smarter buy. It wears like a daily watch rather than a statement piece, which matters if you're picking one watch to rotate through the office, weekend, and water. The 43mm is for committed dive-watch fans or larger wrists who want the full tool-watch presence. Use Underdial to [check how the case dimensions sit against your wrist size](https://underdial.com) before committing, because lug-to-lug is what actually determines fit, and 49mm hangs over most sub-7" wrists.

## Where does the Tissot Seastar fall short? Be honest.

The Seastar is genuinely good, but there are compromises worth knowing before you spend up to $875.

**The bracelet.** The Seastar falls into the same trap that catches a lot of mainstream Swiss brands: the clasp's half-link system makes fine sizing tricky. You're often choosing between slightly loose and slightly tight, which is a frustrating experience on a watch that otherwise feels premium. The 40mm's bracelet has quick-release spring bars, which helps somewhat.

**The polished centre links.** The finish reads dressier than the dive specs warrant. If you want a pure tool-watch look, the polished links will feel at odds with the 300m rating. It's a matter of taste, but it comes up often enough to be worth flagging.

**The 43mm case size.** It's genuinely large. Both references have unidirectional rotating bezels with diving timers, but 49mm lug-to-lug is a real consideration for most wrists. Not a deal-breaker, but verify before buying online.

None of these are reasons not to buy it. They're the specific reasons to choose the 40mm over the 43mm, and to swap the bracelet for a rubber strap if you're actually diving.

## Is the Tissot Seastar a good first watch, or is it a second or third purchase?

This is an underrated question. The Seastar sits at the top of the under-$1,000 bracket, so it's worth asking whether a first-time buyer gets maximum value here, or whether they'd be better served starting lower.

Our honest take: the Seastar rewards buyers who already know they want a dive-watch aesthetic for daily wear, not just water use. Reviewers who love dive watches, even desk divers, consistently find the black-dial Seastar's design punches above its price class. If that's you, $625-$750 is a smarter long-term purchase than cycling through two or three cheaper watches to reach the same destination.

If you're a true beginner who hasn't settled on a style yet, consider the [Seiko 5 Sports](https://underdial.com/articles/is-seiko-5-worth-buying) or [Orient Kamasu](https://underdial.com/articles/is-orient-kamasu-worth-buying) first. Both are in the $200-$300 range and give you the dive-watch experience with less commitment. If you've owned a few watches and know you want Swiss automatic quality in a proper diver, the Seastar is a confident buy, with only minor nitpicks around the bezel insert and clasp adjustability.

Underdial can help you check whether the Seastar overlaps with something you already own, particularly if you're considering it alongside a Seiko Prospex or Certina, so you're not buying redundant water resistance or duplicating a 40mm case you already have on the wrist.

## Verdict: should you buy the Tissot Seastar 1000 in 2026?

**Yes, with a clear recommendation for the 40mm Powermatic 80 reference at around $625-$750.**

What keeps the Tissot proposition solid in 2026 is the spec sheet you get for the money: a Swiss-made movement with an 80-hour reserve and Nivachron anti-magnetic balance spring, sapphire crystal, applied indices, and 300m water resistance, all under $1,000. The Seastar adds ISO 6425 dive certification on top of that. It's a legitimate Swiss diver with heritage behind it, not just a hype piece.

The bracelet gripes are real but fixable. The polished finish is a matter of taste. The 40mm case is genuinely versatile. For anyone wanting their first (or defining) Swiss automatic dive watch under $1,000, the Tissot Seastar 1000 Powermatic 80 is one of the clearest endorsements in this price bracket.

**Skip it if:** you want a fully brushed, utilitarian tool aesthetic (look at the Certina DS Action), your wrist is under 6.5" and the 40mm still runs large, or you already own a dive watch and are simply drawn to the name. **Buy it if:** you want maximum Swiss specs per dollar in a 300m diver, and you're done researching.

## FAQs

**What is the Tissot Seastar 1000 Powermatic 80's price in 2026?**
Depending on the retailer and reference, the Seastar 1000 Powermatic 80 retails between roughly $625 and $875 USD in 2026, with the 40mm version typically at the lower end and the 43mm on a bracelet closer to the top.

**Is the Tissot Seastar 1000 good enough for actual scuba diving?**
Yes. With 300m water resistance, a screw-down crown, ISO 6425 compliance, a unidirectional rotating bezel, and Super-LumiNova lume, it meets the technical requirements for recreational scuba diving, not just desk-diving.

**How does the Tissot Seastar compare to the Seiko Prospex?**
Both sit around $500-$900 and offer 200-300m water resistance. The Seastar wins on power reserve (80 hrs vs ~70 hrs for most Prospex with the 6R35) and crystal quality. The Prospex often wins on lume brightness, aftermarket culture, and brand personality in the diver niche.

**What are the main downsides of the Tissot Seastar 1000?**
The bracelet on the standard model lacks micro-adjustment and can feel loose at half-link increments. The 43mm case size runs large for smaller wrists. And the polished centre links attract scratches faster than a fully-brushed tool-watch aesthetic would.

**What is the difference between the Tissot Seastar 1000 and the Seastar 2000?**

The Seastar 2000 Professional is rated to 600m and comes in a 46mm case with the same Powermatic 80 movement, starting from around $750. The 1000 is the more versatile everyday option; the 2000 is for buyers who want a dedicated professional-spec diver.
