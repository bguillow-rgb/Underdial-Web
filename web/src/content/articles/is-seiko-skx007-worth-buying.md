The Seiko SKX007 was discontinued in 2019, which means every conversation about buying one now starts with a different question than it used to. It's no longer "is it good value at $200?" It's "is it still worth $350 used, when better-spec'd new watches exist for the same money?" That's the question this article actually answers.

## Wait -- the SKX007 is discontinued? When did that happen?

*A question we hear often:* yes, and the timing surprises a lot of buyers who discover the SKX through forums or YouTube rabbit holes.

Seiko officially discontinued the SKX007 in 2019, citing EU movement certification requirements and a strategic shift toward the Prospex family.

Secondary prices climbed, communities intensified, and the SKX007 quietly became a collector piece. An unworn example is now difficult to find at anything close to original retail.

The SKX007 originally retailed for $200, $250 and ran the 7S26 movement, a workhorse automatic with no hacking and no hand-winding. Those limitations felt acceptable at a sub-$200 price. At today's used prices, they're a more serious conversation.

## So what does the SKX007 actually cost on the used market right now?

*This one comes up a lot.* The price depends heavily on condition, completeness, and whether the watch has been modified.

Early 2026 pricing for a clean, unmodified SKX007 on a rubber strap runs $250, $350. A full set with the original Seiko Jubilee bracelet and box adds $50, $100. New-old-stock units occasionally cross $500, but they're scarce.

In 2,484 eBay sales of pre-owned Seiko watches from August through November 2026, unmodified SKX divers sold for a median of $310, with the middle half of transactions falling between $238 and $374 across 270 sales. That real-world data is more useful than any asking-price screenshot. The SKX is not cheap anymore.

## Is the SKX007 actually worth that much compared to what's new right now?

This is the crux of it, and the honest answer is: probably not on pure specification value, but possibly yes for other reasons.

The SKX's value equation has shifted. At its sub-$200 street price a decade ago, the watch was an undisputed bargain. At today's $300, $400 range, you're paying collector markup for a diver that lacks crown winding, offers no regulation adjustment without opening the case, and ships on a rattly pressed-metal bracelet.

The watches that eat the SKX007's lunch on paper at a similar or lower price in 2026 include:

If your goal is a capable daily tool watch rather than a specific piece of history, those alternatives represent better value than hunting for discontinued SKX007 units at inflated prices.

## Then who *should* still buy an SKX007?

*Readers frequently ask:* "If the specs don't beat the competition, is there any reason to actually buy one?" Yes, a few clear ones.

**You want the original.** There is genuine character in the SKX case that the SRPD approximates but doesn't replicate exactly. The slightly rounder proportions, the original handset, the specific way the case wears, these things matter to some buyers. If you find a clean example at a fair price on the secondary market, the 200m water resistance, proven case, and near-indestructible movement make it a legitimate daily wearer and dive companion. Discontinuation changes nothing about its functionality.

**You appreciate collector context.** Few watch brands command the kind of cult following that Seiko has built in the dive watch world, from the SKX007 that defined affordable diving to the modern Prospex line. Owning the watch that started that conversation for a generation of enthusiasts carries a kind of meaning that a spec sheet doesn't capture.

**You're a modifier.** The SKX platform has one of the deepest aftermarket ecosystems in watchmaking: dials, bezels, movements, cases. If you plan to build rather than just wear, the SKX case is still a legitimate starting platform.

Who should *not* buy one: anyone primarily chasing specifications per dollar, anyone who wants a manufacturer warranty, or anyone who isn't prepared to do careful due diligence on a used purchase.

## What should I check before buying a used SKX007?

*A question we hear often:* the used market has its share of heavily modified, poorly described, or outright misrepresented SKX watches. Here's a practical pre-purchase checklist:

- **Movement:** Ask for a video of the seconds sweep and the date flip at midnight. A healthy 7S26 sweeps smoothly and changes date cleanly.
- **Bezel:** Should click firmly in one direction only, with no backward play. A loose or slipping bezel is a safety concern if you actually dive.
- **Crown:** Must screw down completely and smoothly. Stripped crown threads are a common issue on used dive watches.
- **Crystal:** The original Hardlex scratches. Surface marks are expected; deep gouges or crazing indicate harder abuse.
- **Caseback:** Should show Seiko's wave pattern and the serial number. Mismatched casebacks suggest parts-watch history.
- **Reference number:** Confirm it matches the dial colour. Black dial is 007; blue/red "Pepsi" bezel is 009.

Reputable platforms for used SKX purchases include WatchUSeek and Reddit's r/Watchexchange for peer verification, eBay for the largest selection (use authentication services for purchases over $400), and Chrono24 for buyer protection, though prices there run 10%, 15% higher.

## How does the SKX007 compare specifically to the Seiko 5 Sports SRPD?

This is the most common direct comparison, because the SRPD inherited the SKX's case shape when Seiko pulled the original. The silhouette is familiar, but the differences are real.

The SRPD runs the 4R36 movement, which adds hacking (the seconds hand stops when you pull the crown) and hand-winding, both absent on the SKX's 7S26. On water resistance, though, the SKX wins: 200m with a screw-down crown and full ISO 6425 certification, versus the SRPD's 100m with a push-pull crown and no ISO rating. The cheaper (older) watch is actually the one you can take diving.

The SRPD costs less new than a good-condition used SKX. If daily convenience matters, and for most buyers it does, the SRPD's hacking and hand-winding are genuinely useful. If dive capability matters, the SKX is the better tool.

## Does the SKX007 hold its value if I change my mind later?

Better than almost anything else in this price bracket. The SKX007 has roughly doubled in value since discontinuation. Unmodded SKX divers sold for a median of $310 on eBay from August through November 2026, with the middle half falling between $238 and $374 across 270 sales. A buyer who paid $250 for a clean example two years ago and sells now is likely to break even or better.

That said, resale is not a reason to buy a watch. It's a tiebreaker. The collector premium on the SKX is real, but it's conditional on the example being unmodified, complete, and in honest condition. A heavily modded SKX or one missing its bracelet and box sells at a significant discount from median. Pre-owned prices have risen well above original retail since the 2019 discontinuation, so value depends entirely on your reason for buying.

## FAQs

**Is the Seiko SKX007 still being made?**
No. Seiko discontinued the SKX007 in 2019 after a production run that began in 1996. It is only available on the secondary (used) market.

**How much does the Seiko SKX007 cost in 2026?**
Clean, unmodified examples on a rubber strap typically sell for $250, $350. A full set with the original Seiko Jubilee bracelet and box adds $50, $100 to that figure.

**What is the best modern replacement for the SKX007?**
From Seiko's own lineup, the Seiko 5 Sports SRPD inherits the case shape and adds hacking and hand-winding at around $275, $325 new. The Seiko Prospex SRPE93 Turtle is the best ISO-certified choice under $500, with 200m water resistance, the 4R36 automatic movement, and an iconic cushion case design. The Orient Kamasu adds sapphire crystal and ISO certification for under $300.

**Does the Seiko SKX007 hold its value?**
In 2,484 eBay sales from August through November 2026, SKX divers sold for a median of $310, well above the original retail of $200, $250.

**Should I buy a modified (modded) SKX007?**
Only if you understand exactly what was changed and can inspect it. Mods can add value or complicate authentication. If resale value matters, unmodified examples are strongly preferable.

**Is the SKX007's 7S26 movement a problem?**
It lacks hacking and hand-winding, inconvenient but not a dealbreaker for daily wear. Accuracy is typical for the class: +-15, 20 seconds per day. Seiko service centers still support it, which matters for long-term ownership.
