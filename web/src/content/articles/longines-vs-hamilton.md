Both Hamilton and Longines sit inside the Swatch Group family, share access to the same ETA movement supply chain, and draw on deep Swiss manufacturing history. But they are not the same watch at a different price. They occupy genuinely different rungs, and which one deserves your money depends almost entirely on whether you can stretch past $1,000 and whether that stretch buys you something you'll actually feel on the wrist every day.

---

## Wait -- are Hamilton and Longines actually related?

*A question we hear often:* and worth clearing up before anything else, because the answer shapes everything downstream.

Both are Swiss-made brands under the Swatch Group umbrella, sharing corporate parentage and access to the same ETA movement supply chain, yet they occupy different rungs on the prestige and pricing ladder.

Hamilton is positioned as an accessible entry into Swiss mechanical watchmaking, blending American heritage with Swiss manufacturing at prices that typically run $400 to $1,500. Longines sits one tier above, drawing on nearly two centuries of watchmaking history and more refined finishing to justify prices from $1,000 to $4,500.

That shared parentage matters for one reason: you are not choosing between a cheap movement and a premium one in any absolute sense. You are choosing how much of your money goes toward refinement, finishing, brand prestige, and COSC accuracy, versus raw Swiss mechanical value at a lower price point.

---

## So what does Hamilton actually cost, and what do you get?

The Hamilton Khaki Field Auto ($575) is one of the most celebrated affordable field watches ever made. It runs the Powermatic 80, an ETA-based movement with an 80-hour power reserve -- over three full days -- which is exceptional at this price and beats most competitors in the segment. Add sapphire crystal and genuine military heritage and the value case is hard to argue with.

Hamilton was established over 130 years ago making pocket watches to time locomotive trains and prevent accidents. They went on to supply the U.S. military with chronographs and field watches through both World Wars. That history is not marketing copy; it shows up directly in the Khaki Field's legible dial, 24-hour secondary markings, and no-nonsense tool-watch proportions.

The Khaki Field collection starts from roughly $525, and pre-owned examples trade from $350 to $800, making them some of the better entry points into Swiss watchmaking at any price. For buyers inside the $1,000 ceiling, Hamilton is not a compromise. It is a deliberate destination.

## And what does Longines cost -- and where does it sit relative to $1,000?

*This one comes up a lot:* specifically whether Longines is reachable under $1,000 at all.

Honestly, it's tight. The Khaki Field Auto at $575 versus the Longines Spirit at $2,300 is a fourfold price gap for watches that share the same corporate parent and draw from the same ETA supply chain. The Spirit is genuinely excellent, but it is not a sub-$1,000 conversation at current retail.

Where Longines can enter the under-$1,000 bracket is the pre-owned market. The median market value for Hamilton sits at $897 across 651 references; Longines carries a median of $1,800 across 1,327 references. Patient shoppers with time to hunt grey-market or lightly used pieces can occasionally find a Longines Conquest or an older automatic reference nudging under $1,000, but it takes effort and flexibility on model choice.

The practical answer: buying new at retail, Hamilton owns the under-$1,000 Swiss automatic category. Open to pre-owned, Longines becomes a stretch goal worth targeting.

## What are the real movement differences between the two brands?

*Readers frequently ask:* whether the Longines movement is meaningfully better, or whether the marketing just makes it sound that way.

Here is the honest breakdown:

| Feature | Hamilton Khaki Field Auto | Longines Spirit Auto |
|---|---|---|
| Movement | Powermatic 80 (ETA-based) | Calibre L888 (ETA-based) |
| Power Reserve | 80 hours | 64 hours |
| COSC Certified | No | Yes (~+/-4 s/day) |
| Silicon Hairspring | No | Yes |
| Accuracy (typical) | +/-10-15 s/day | +/-4-6 s/day |
| Retail Price (2026) | ~$525-$945 | ~$1,625-$2,300+ |
| Sapphire Crystal | Yes | Yes |
| Water Resistance | 100m | 100m |
| Case Finishing | Good brushed/polished | Excellent; finer grain |

If accuracy matters to you, the Longines is COSC-certified and that may be worth the difference. Otherwise, the Hamilton is the practical choice, and the Spirit is arguably overpriced for what it delivers in purely functional terms. The Hamilton's 80-hour power reserve beats the Spirit's 64 hours; both are comfortable for daily wear.

The silicon hairspring in the Longines L888 is a real technical advantage -- it makes the movement more resistant to magnetic fields and temperature changes -- but for someone wearing a watch to work and on weekends, rather than inside an MRI machine, the practical difference is small. The COSC accuracy gain matters most if you are precision-conscious or find yourself setting your watch daily.

## Is the Longines finishing actually worth twice the price?

This is where the gap is most honestly felt. If you know what you want and the Spirit's price is not a problem, go for the Spirit -- the finish is meaningfully better, the movement is a step up, and the raised markers with their lume are excellent. That is not spin. The case chamfering, dial texture, and bracelet engineering on Longines do sit above Hamilton's. You feel it when you handle both.

The Spirit is the more refined watch by a clear margin. The Khaki Field is roughly one-quarter the price and delivers about 90 percent of the daily-wearing satisfaction. For pure value, the Khaki Field is almost impossible to beat.

The 10 percent you give up is real, but it lives in the periphery: the feel of the clasp, the depth of the brushing on the case sides, the measured smoothness of the bracelet links. That last 10 percent costs more than the first 90 percent combined. For many buyers, that math does not work. For buyers who have already owned a Hamilton and want to feel the upgrade, it absolutely does.

## Which specific models should I actually compare head-to-head?

*A question we hear often:* because neither brand is a single watch. Here are the most meaningful matchups for buyers under, or close to, the $1,000 mark.

**Hamilton Khaki Field Auto (38mm, ~$525) vs Longines Spirit Zulu Time (~$1,625+):** Not a fair fight financially, but the most apples-to-apples comparison stylistically. Both are field/pilot-inspired, military-heritage, brushed-case automatics. The Spirit is the more refined watch. The Khaki Field is the smarter spend at its price.

**Hamilton Khaki Field Murph (~$895-$945) vs Longines HydroConquest (~$1,350):** Different categories -- the Murph is a dress-adjacent field watch; the HydroConquest is a dive watch -- but both represent the upper edge of each brand's accessible tier. The HydroConquest brings a ceramic bezel, sunburst dial, and the L888 movement in a well-proportioned 41mm case. It's a capable piece, but it sits $400+ above the Murph.

**Hamilton Jazzmaster Open Heart (~$995) vs Longines Record (~$2,200+):** For buyers who want an exhibition dial and dress-watch character, the Jazzmaster is one of the better sub-$1,000 Swiss options with genuine visual presence. The Longines Record offers better finishing at roughly twice the cost.

If you are coming up from a Seiko or Orient and moving toward your first Swiss piece, Hamilton is the natural step. ## Does one brand hold its value better?

Based on real transaction data, Hamilton showed a stronger 1-year average return of +2.7% compared to Longines at -3.3%. Around 50% of Hamilton references appreciated over the past year, versus 55.3% for Longines. The picture is nuanced: Longines has more references appreciating as a share, but Hamilton's overall trajectory was positive in recent data while Longines was slightly negative, likely reflecting higher price-point sensitivity in a tighter market.

The honest take: neither brand under $1,500 is a meaningful investment vehicle. Buying a watch as an investment is a mistake. Buy it because it connects you to something -- a particular era, a craft, an aesthetic that fits who you are. Value retention is a tiebreaker, not the primary reason.

---

## The verdict: which one should you actually buy?

If your budget ceiling is $1,000, buy the **Hamilton Khaki Field Auto**. It is one of the most complete Swiss automatic watches at this price, wears well on both leather and NATO straps, and leaves room to build out the rest of your collection. You can always move to Longines later, and when you do, you will appreciate the upgrade more for having owned the Hamilton first.

If your budget can flex to $1,200-$1,500 and you are open to pre-owned, a used **Longines HydroConquest or Conquest** is worth hunting for. The finishing is genuinely better, the L888 movement is a real technical step up, and Longines carries more weight in most rooms. A seasoned collector, or someone whose attention to detail borders on the obsessive, will prefer the Spirit. The prestige is real, and so is the resale position.

Whether Longines' advantages in finishing, silicon hairsprings, and brand prestige justify the premium comes down to your budget and priorities. For many buyers, Hamilton is where Swiss quality and genuine affordability actually meet. That is still the most accurate summary anyone has written on this matchup.

## FAQs

**Are Longines and Hamilton owned by the same company?**
Yes. Both brands sit under the Swatch Group umbrella, which gives them access to the same ETA movement supply chain. Longines occupies a higher prestige tier, reflected in its pricing and finishing quality.

**Is Longines really better than Hamilton?**
By most objective measures -- case finishing, movement accuracy (COSC certification on key models), and silicon hairsprings -- Longines is a step above. Whether that step is worth roughly twice the price depends on your budget and how much you care about those refinements day-to-day.

**What is the best Hamilton watch to buy under $1,000?**
The Khaki Field Auto (38mm, ~$525) is the consensus favourite for buyers under $1,000. The Murph edition (~$895-$945) is also worth considering if you want a larger case and a culturally distinctive dial. Both use the Powermatic 80 movement with an 80-hour power reserve.

**Can you buy a Longines automatic watch for under $1,000?**
It's difficult but not impossible. Some older Conquest models, a handful of grey-market new references, and pre-owned Longines watches can dip below $1,000. The brand's modern automatic line generally starts at $1,000-$1,200 new at retail, with the Spirit sitting well above that.

**Which brand holds its value better -- Hamilton or Longines?**
According to watch market tracking data, Hamilton showed a stronger 1-year average return than Longines in recent figures, though Longines has a higher proportion of references appreciating year-over-year. Neither brand under $1,500 is a reliable investment vehicle -- buy what you'll wear consistently.
