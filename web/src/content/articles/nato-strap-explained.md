A NATO strap is one of the cheapest, most useful upgrades you can make to any watch under $1,000, and also one of the most misunderstood. Buy a Seiko 5, Hamilton Khaki Field, or virtually any sport or field watch and you'll quickly hit the same question: should you ditch the stock bracelet or leather strap for a NATO? This guide answers that honestly.

## So what exactly is a NATO strap, and where did the name come from?

The NATO strap is a single-piece nylon band that threads all the way through and under the watch case, looping around both spring bars rather than clipping onto them separately. Woven from dense nylon or polyester, it passes underneath the watch so the case stays secured even if one spring bar fails.

The name traces back to a British Ministry of Defence supply designation. The design started as a British military issue, originally called the G10 after the supply form used to requisition one, worn by UK forces from the 1970s onward. From there it spread into the broader watch community and became a staple for Seiko modders, field watch enthusiasts, and anyone who wants a quick, low-cost way to refresh a look.

The key design detail, and the reason people swear by these things, is security. If one of your watch's spring bars breaks (a common failure point when a watch gets snagged or dropped), the watch stays attached to the other spring bar and doesn't go anywhere. On a standard two-piece leather or rubber strap, a broken spring bar means your watch hits the ground, or the bottom of the ocean.

## Does a NATO strap actually fit differently than a regular strap?

*A question we hear often:* yes, and it matters more on some watches than others.

A traditional double-pass NATO puts two layers of nylon fabric underneath the watch case, lifting it off the wrist and making the watch wear thicker. A single-pass strap cuts out that extra layer, letting the watch sit closer to your wrist.

For a 44mm diver or a chunky field watch, that extra 1-2mm of height is barely noticeable. For a slim 38mm dress watch, it can feel significant. If you care about case thickness (and it's worth understanding when buying any watch under $1,000), try a single-pass NATO first on slimmer cases.

Fit also depends on getting the lug width right. The strap has to match your watch's lug width exactly: 18mm, 20mm, and 22mm cover the vast majority of watches in this price range. If you're unsure, the lug width is listed in every watch spec sheet and in the Underdial app when you look up a model.

## NATO vs leather vs rubber, which strap material should I actually pick?

*This one comes up a lot:* the honest answer is that each material has a genuinely different job, and none of them is universally better.

| Strap type | Best for | Water resistance | Lifespan (daily wear) | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NATO (nylon) | Casual, travel, outdoors | Good, dries fast | 1-3 years | $10-$40 |
| Leather | Office, dress, evenings | Poor, avoid getting wet | 1-3 years with care | $30-$120 |
| Rubber (FKM) | Sport, diving, gym | Excellent | 3-5 years | $30-$100 |
| Steel bracelet | Everyday, all-rounder | Excellent | 10+ years | $30-$150 |

Hiking, kayaking, cycling, gym sessions: NATO or rubber. Of the two, NATO generally wins for general outdoor use since it breathes and dries out in under 30 minutes. Rubber pulls ahead specifically for water sports.

For formal occasions, leather, no exceptions. Weddings, black-tie dinners, gallery openings: any event with a dress code above "smart casual" calls for fine leather. Show up in a NATO and it looks like you forgot to change. Show up in rubber and it looks like you're headed straight to a triathlon.

For daily office and commute wear, leather or steel bracelet tends to win on overall polish. But the real trick with a cheap NATO is keeping one in your bag and swapping it in two minutes when the weekend starts.

## Can I put a NATO strap on any watch?

*Readers frequently ask:* almost, but there are a couple of exceptions worth knowing.

Any watch with standard removable spring bar lugs can take a NATO strap, which covers the vast majority of affordable watches from Seiko, Orient, Citizen, Hamilton, Tissot, and virtually every microbrand. You need a spring bar tool (under $10), two minutes, and the right lug width.

The main exceptions are **integrated-bracelet watches**, where the bracelet is designed as part of the case. The Tissot PRX, for instance, uses a proprietary integrated bracelet that cannot be swapped for a standard strap of any kind without modification. That's one of the tradeoffs of the integrated-bracelet style that's worth weighing before you buy, and something we dig into in our [integrated bracelet watches guide](/are-integrated-bracelet-watches-worth-buying).

Watches with recessed lugs or unusual lug shapes (some Casio G-Shocks, for example) may also need adapter hardware. But for 90% of watches in this price segment, a NATO swap is completely straightforward.

## Are NATO straps worth it, or is this just a Reddit trend?

Genuinely worth it, for most watch buyers under $1,000, for two specific reasons.

**First: cost per enjoyment.** A quality NATO strap costs $10-$30. NATO and fabric straps are the cheapest, toughest option for daily rotation, and they will outlive the watch on your wrist. That means you can buy three or four NATO straps in different colors (olive, navy, black, grey) and effectively have four different watches for the cost of a mediocre lunch. For anyone building a watch rotation on a budget, this is one of the highest-ROI moves available.

**Second: the spring bar safety argument is real.** Most people never experience a spring bar failure, but those who have watched a beloved $400 watch hit concrete regret not using a NATO. The dual-bar threading design is legitimate insurance that costs nothing once you own the strap.

The "Reddit trend" framing is fair only in the sense that NATO straps have been overcelebrated by people who put them on watches that look odd with them. A slim dress watch on a wide NATO looks jarring. Match the strap to the watch's character: a tool watch, field watch, or dive watch on a NATO looks correct. A Seiko Presage Cocktail Time on a NATO looks like a mistake.

One practical tip: if you rotate straps seasonally (leather in fall and winter, NATO or rubber in spring and summer), each strap lasts noticeably longer because it gets rest time. That's genuinely good advice for anyone who owns more than one strap.

## How do I actually put a NATO strap on a watch?

The process takes under five minutes with a proper spring bar tool. Do not use a knife or screwdriver; you'll scratch the case.

1. Remove both spring bars using the forked end of the spring bar tool.
2. Slide the NATO strap's short tail end (the half with just the keeper ring) through one side of the lug gap.
3. Pass the strap under the caseback.
4. Thread through the second lug gap.
5. Bring the long tail over the top of the case, through the hardware buckle and keeper rings.
6. Reinsert both spring bars to lock the strap in place.

The strap goes on without reinstalling the spring bars in the case. The NATO threads around them, so the spring bars just hold the strap in its channel. This is the detail that confuses most first-timers. Once you've done it once, it takes about 90 seconds.

## What NATO strap width do I need for my watch?

Look up your watch's lug width before you order. The lug width is the distance in millimeters between the two lugs, the prongs that extend from the case. It's a standard spec listed on every manufacturer page. The most common sizes for watches under $1,000 are:

- **20mm** -- Seiko 5 Sports (most models), Hamilton Khaki Field, Orient Kamasu, Tissot T-Classic series
- **22mm** -- many larger dive watches, some Seiko Prospex models
- **18mm** -- smaller dress watches, some vintage-style pieces

If the width isn't printed anywhere, use a ruler or calipers to measure between the lugs directly. Ordering one size off means the strap either won't fit or will fit loosely, so it matters.

## FAQs

**What is the difference between a NATO strap and a regular watch strap?**
A regular two-piece strap attaches at two separate points, one spring bar per end. A NATO strap is one continuous piece of nylon that passes under the watch case and loops through both spring bars, so the watch stays on your wrist even if one spring bar snaps.

**Can I put a NATO strap on any watch?**
Almost any watch with standard removable spring bar lugs can take a NATO strap: just match the lug width. Integrated-bracelet watches (like the Tissot PRX) are the main exception.

**Do NATO straps make a watch sit higher on the wrist?**
Yes. A traditional double-pass NATO adds roughly 1-2mm of height because the nylon passes under the case. Single-pass NATO straps minimize this. It's more noticeable on slim dress watches than on chunky divers or field watches.

**How long does a NATO strap last?**
With daily wear, a quality nylon NATO typically lasts 1-3 years before the nylon frays or the hardware tarnishes. Since a good NATO costs $10-$30, replacement is rarely a concern. It's arguably the most cost-effective consumable in watch ownership.

**Is a NATO strap suitable for swimming or diving?**
NATO straps are fine for casual water exposure and swimming; they dry in under 30 minutes. For serious diving, most divers prefer a rubber or FKM strap, which is more streamlined and doesn't absorb water at all.

**What's the difference between a NATO and a Zulu strap?**
The Zulu is a NATO adaptation using a thicker fabric weave and more substantial metal hardware, with rounded loops and an oval-shaped buckle. In practice, Zulu straps look slightly chunkier; NATO straps sit a little flatter. Both work the same way and take the same sizing.

**Should I use a NATO strap on a dress watch?**
Generally not. A NATO strap suits casual, sport, field, and dive watches well. On a slim dress watch it looks out of place and adds unwanted height. For dress watches, a smooth leather strap is the right call.
