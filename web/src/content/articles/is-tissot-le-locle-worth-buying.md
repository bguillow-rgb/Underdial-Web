The Tissot Le Locle sits in a quietly contested corner of the sub-$500 watch market: Swiss automatic, formal aesthetic, sapphire crystal, guilloché dial -- specs that would have cost twice as much a decade ago. But "worth buying" is never just a spec-sheet question. It depends on who you are, what you already own, and what you're actually going to wear it with.

## "Is the Le Locle actually Swiss-made, or is that just marketing?"

*A question we hear often:*

Tissot traces its roots to Le Locle, Switzerland, where the company was founded in 1853. The Le Locle watch line is literally named after that town, and the Swiss Made designation is legitimate. The current Le Locle Powermatic 80 runs the ETA C07.111 movement and retails for around $475. Tissot is part of the Swatch Group.

That matters because Swatch Group's movement supply gives Tissot access to genuinely well-engineered calibers at scale -- something most brands at this price simply can't match. The ETA C07.111 (marketed as the Powermatic 80) is a proven, serviceable movement with decades of production behind it. You are not buying a rebranded generic movement. You are buying architecture that runs in watches costing three times as much.

## "What exactly does the dial look like -- I keep seeing 'guilloché' but I don't know what that means in practice."

Guilloché is engine-turned engraving: a repeating geometric pattern cut into the metal dial surface that catches light at different angles as your wrist moves. On paper it sounds fussy. In person it looks like something from a watchmaker charging $3,000.

Tissot managed to put this on a $475 watch. The hands and Roman numerals shift between silver and black depending on the light. The 39.3mm case is slim enough to disappear under a shirt cuff.

This is not a watch that screams for attention across the room. It earns a second look at close range -- which is exactly the point of a dress watch.

The 80-hour power reserve is a genuine everyday convenience. Take it off Friday night, put it back on Monday morning, and it's still running. That's a real upgrade over older ETA movements rated at 38-42 hours, and it's one of the more practical reasons to choose this watch over cheaper alternatives.

## "So who is this watch actually for -- and who should skip it?"

*This one comes up a lot:*

The Le Locle is for someone who regularly wears a suit, blazer, or smart dress and wants a Swiss automatic that looks like it costs significantly more than it does. It's an office watch, a wedding watch, a dinner watch.

Who should skip it? Anyone who needs a watch that keeps up with an active lifestyle. The design is traditional by intention, and if you find that conservative, nothing here will change your mind. At 50m water resistance it handles hand-washing and getting caught in the rain, but you should not be swimming in it or wearing it to the gym.

If your life is mostly casual -- jeans, t-shirts, outdoor weekends -- the [Seiko Presage](https://underdial.com/articles/is-seiko-presage-worth-buying) or the [Tissot PRX](https://underdial.com/articles/is-tissot-prx-worth-buying) will serve you better day-to-day.

## "How does the Le Locle compare to the Tissot PRX -- they're similar prices, what's the actual difference?"

*Readers frequently ask:*

Both live near $475. Both are Swiss automatic. Both have sapphire crystals. But they are genuinely different watches for genuinely different buyers.

| Factor | Tissot Le Locle PM80 | Tissot PRX PM80 |
|---|---|---|
| Aesthetic | Classic dress, guilloché dial | Sport-elegant, integrated bracelet |
| Strap/bracelet | Leather with deployment clasp | Integrated steel bracelet |
| Case size | 39.3 mm | 40 mm |
| Water resistance | 50 m | 100 m |
| Best occasion | Formal, office, gifting | Office, casual, weekends |
| Versatility | Narrower | Broader |

The PRX was designed as a versatile daily driver, drawing from the sport-luxury integrated-bracelet look pioneered by watches costing ten times as much. The Le Locle draws from pure European dress watchmaking. Neither is objectively better. They answer different questions. If someone asks "which Tissot automatic under $500 should I get?" the real answer is: what are you mostly wearing it with?

If you already own a sport or casual watch and need something formal, the Le Locle fills that gap cleanly. If you want one watch that does everything, buy the PRX. Tools like [Underdial](https://underdial.com) can run a quick check against what you already own to show whether the Le Locle complements your collection or duplicates it -- useful if you're on the fence.

## "Does the Le Locle hold its value if I want to sell it later?"

The honest answer: not particularly well, and that's true of almost every sub-$500 watch. You're paying for the watch itself, not resale value. Tissot is accessible by design, and the secondary market reflects that. Expect 25-40% depreciation from retail once worn -- that's the reality of the category.

The right frame here is that you're paying around $475 for years of genuine enjoyment from a Swiss mechanical watch, not for an asset.

According to secondary market data, Tissot Le Locle automatics typically sell used for $250-$350 in excellent condition, depending on model year and whether the original box is included. If that delta bothers you, buying pre-owned at that price is actually the smarter entry point.

## "Are there cheaper watches that deliver the same thing?"

*A question we hear often:*

Genuinely, yes -- a few. The [Orient Bambino](https://underdial.com/articles/seiko-5-vs-orient-bambino) offers a comparable dress-watch aesthetic with an in-house Orient automatic for around $180-$250. Its movement isn't as refined and the finishing is a step down, but for a first dress watch it's hard to fault. The [Seiko Presage](https://underdial.com/articles/is-seiko-presage-worth-buying) line also competes on dial artistry -- some models feature enamel, urushi lacquer, or patterned dials that rival the Le Locle's guilloché at similar or slightly lower prices.

What the Le Locle offers that neither quite matches is the combination of Swiss provenance, the Powermatic 80's 80-hour power reserve, sapphire crystal, and that guilloché dial -- all in one package under $500. If those four things matter to you together, the Le Locle is essentially uncontested at its price. If you'd trade Swiss origin for more dial variety, the Seiko Presage is worth serious consideration.

## FAQs

**What movement does the Tissot Le Locle use?**
Current Le Locle models use Tissot's Powermatic 80 caliber (a modified ETA C07.111), offering 80 hours of power reserve, hacking, and hand-winding. Older models used the standard ETA 2824-2.

**How water resistant is the Tissot Le Locle?**
The Le Locle is rated to 50 metres (5 ATM). That's enough for hand-washing and rain, but it's not a dive or sport watch. Keep it out of the pool.

**Is the Tissot Le Locle good for everyday wear?**
It depends on your lifestyle. If your days involve an office, dinners, or formal occasions, yes -- it handles daily wear well. If you need something rugged, look at the Seiko Presage or Tissot Gentleman instead.

**What is the Tissot Le Locle case size?**
The standard Le Locle Powermatic 80 measures 39.3mm in diameter, roughly 11mm thick. It sits slim on the wrist and disappears under a shirt cuff -- which is exactly the point.

**How does the Tissot Le Locle compare to the Tissot PRX?**
The Le Locle is a classic dress watch -- guilloché dial, leather strap, formal aesthetic. The PRX is a sport-elegant integrated-bracelet watch built for versatile daily wear. If you wear suits, the Le Locle. If you want one watch for everything, the PRX.
