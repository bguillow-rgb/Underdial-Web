Three Orient dive watches. Same movement, same 200m water resistance, and a price gap of maybe $50 between the cheapest and the most specced-up. And yet the "Mako vs Ray" debate is one of the most reliably recurring threads on every affordable-watch forum going. Not because they're confusing -- because they're *so* close that the choice feels high-stakes. This article cuts through it.

## What's actually the same across all three?

Before getting into differences, it's worth nailing what you're buying regardless of which model you pick.

The Orient Mako, Ray, and Kamasu share the same foundational architecture: stainless steel cases with screw-down crowns, unidirectional rotating bezels with diving scales, luminous dials and hands, and in-house Orient automatic movements. All three offer 200m water resistance. That rating is meaningful -- it clears the bar for recreational scuba diving, not just splash resistance.

Orient introduced the Ray in 2004 as a 200m water-resistant sports watch powered by the brand's in-house automatic movement, in a stainless steel case with unidirectional bezel. The Mako followed a similar path and has since become one of the most-recommended starter automatics in the hobby -- the reference that defined what a sub-$150 automatic dive watch could be.

## So what actually separates the Mako II from the Ray II?

*A question we hear often:* people assume there must be a technical reason to choose one over the other. There mostly isn't.

The most obvious difference is the markers and hands. The Mako II has bar-shaped markers and pointier hands; the Ray II does not. The Mako also sports Arabic 3-6-9 numerals on the dial, which the Ray skips entirely, and the bracelet swaps the Ray's polished centre links for a more standard brushed oyster design.

In plain terms: the Ray II reads a little cleaner and dressier, thanks to round dot indices and that subtle flash from the polished bracelet links. The Mako II looks more utilitarian -- closer to a classic tool-watch aesthetic, with the Arabic numerals lending it a slightly retro military feel. Neither is better. It's purely a dial-and-bracelet preference call.

Mechanically, both run on Orient's F6922 calibre with a 40-hour power reserve, and both use mineral crystal. The Mako II measures 41.5mm in diameter, 47mm lug-to-lug, 13mm thick, with a 22mm lug width. The Ray II shares virtually the same case dimensions. If you've tried one on, you know exactly how the other will fit.

## Is the Kamasu worth paying more for?

*This one comes up a lot:* once people discover the Kamasu exists, they want to know whether the upgrade is genuine or just marketing.

The Kamasu wins on specification. It gets sapphire crystal over mineral glass, and the hacking F6923 movement over the non-hacking F6922. At a premium of roughly $30-$50 over the Mako at most retailers, those upgrades are directly proportional to the extra cost. The sapphire crystal alone justifies it for anyone expecting to wear the watch daily.

Hacking is worth a word: a hacking movement lets you pull the crown to stop the seconds hand, so you can set the time precisely. The Mako II and Ray II's F6922 doesn't do that. For most people it makes no practical difference day-to-day. But over years of daily wear, sapphire crystal is the more meaningful upgrade -- it resists scratches far better than mineral glass and keeps the watch looking sharper without any extra upkeep.

The Kamasu also adds a power-reserve indicator, and its dial design leans slightly more vintage-inspired, with applied rectangular lume indices. Some buyers prefer that over both the Mako's Arabic numerals and the Ray's round dots. Overall the three models have more similarities than differences -- what you're choosing between is a handful of specific upgrades, not entirely different watches.

## Which one should I actually buy?

*Readers frequently ask:* "Just tell me which one." Fair enough.

Buy the Ray II if you want the cleanest-looking dial for the money. The round indices and polished bracelet links give it a slightly more versatile look that works in casual office settings as well as weekend wear. It's the quieter watch of the two entry-level options.

Buy the Mako II if the 3-6-9 Arabic numeral layout appeals to you. It's a classic tool-watch reference borrowed from vintage military divers and gives the watch more character on the dial. Some buyers find it easier to read at a glance precisely because those numerals give the eye an anchor point.

Buy the Kamasu if you're going to wear this watch every day without babying it. To be fair to the entry-level pair: the Mako's mineral glass and F6922 are not drawbacks at the price -- they're the honest specification for what you're paying. But if the watch is going to be on your wrist six or seven days a week, the Kamasu's sapphire crystal will outlast mineral glass visually by years. The $50 premium is genuinely worth it for daily-wear use.

If you already own a dress watch or a quartz everyday piece and want a sporty second watch, the Ray II or Mako II at around $160 is hard to argue with. It's one of the few places in watchmaking where you get a genuine in-house automatic movement with 200m water resistance for under $200.

## Will these hold up as a daily wearer? What's the real-world durability like?

The Mako II has good build quality and excellent value for money. Forum consensus over years of use backs that up -- these are not delicate dress watches. The screw-down crown, solid caseback, and steel bracelet are all built for actual use.

The weak point commonly noted across all three is the stock bracelet. Quality aftermarket steel bracelets cost $50-$100 on their own, which gives you a sense of where corners were cut at this price. The factory bracelets on the Mako and Ray II are functional but have hollow endlinks and a basic clasp. Many owners upgrade to an aftermarket steel bracelet or swap to rubber or nylon for a better wearing experience. Worth factoring into your total budget if bracelet quality matters to you.

One maintenance note: water resistance isn't permanent -- gaskets age and shocks can deform parts. If you swim or dive regularly with any of these watches, having the water resistance pressure-tested every year or two by a watchmaker is worthwhile, particularly before diving. This applies to all three models equally.

## Does fit differ between models? I have a smaller wrist.

All three divers land in the same physical footprint, so there's no meaningful fit difference between models. The choice here is about specs and dial preference, not sizing.

At 41.5-41.8mm and a 47mm lug-to-lug, these are medium-to-large watches. Lug-to-lug matters more than dial diameter -- a 40mm watch with long lugs can wear larger than a 42mm with compact lugs -- and the Orient divers' 47mm lug-to-lug is a real consideration on wrists under 6.5 inches. Watches designed to fit smaller wrists are harder to find in this category, and mainstream models often ignore the issue entirely.

## Where does this fit in the Orient lineup overall?

Orient has produced mechanical watches since 1950 and is owned by Seiko Epson, which means the in-house movement quality is backed by significant manufacturing infrastructure. The three divers sit at the accessible end of a lineup that includes the dressier Orient Bambino and the more premium Orient Star range -- the latter starting well above $300.
