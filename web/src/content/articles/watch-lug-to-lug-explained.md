Before you spend a dollar on a watch you haven't tried on, there's one spec that matters more than any other -- and most product pages bury it, or skip it entirely. It's not case diameter. It's lug-to-lug. Understanding it takes about two minutes and will save you from one of the most common and most avoidable watch-buying mistakes: ordering a watch that looks great in photos, then discovering it overhangs your wrist like a manhole cover.

---

## Wait -- what even *is* lug-to-lug? Nobody explains this clearly.

Lug-to-lug (often shortened to "L2L") is the case dimension measured from the tip of the upper lug to the tip of the lower lug, parallel to the strap. The lugs are the four prongs that stick out from the case -- two at twelve, two at six -- where the strap attaches.

Think of it this way: case diameter tells you how wide the watch looks from across the room; lug-to-lug tells you how long it sits along your arm. That length decides whether the lugs overhang, whether the case wobbles, and whether you'll actually wear the watch or leave it in the box.

Diameter gets all the attention in watch listings, but lug-to-lug is the number that decides whether a case fits your wrist or hangs off the sides. It's also the spec most often missing from product pages, which causes more online sizing complaints than any other single measurement.

---

## So how is this different from case diameter? Aren't they basically the same number?

*A question we hear often:* no, they are not, and this confusion is exactly where buyers go wrong.

Two 40 mm watches can have lug-to-lug spans of 46 mm and 52 mm and fit completely differently. The dial diameter is identical on paper, but one sits flat on the wrist and one overhangs it. A watch can have a modest 40 mm case but long, drooping lugs that push the total length to 50 mm. On a 6.5-inch wrist, those lugs overhang the flat of your wrist bone and the case pivots when you flex. The watch wears huge. Meanwhile a 42 mm case with short, stubby lugs and a 46 mm lug-to-lug sits flat. Same diameter, completely different fit.

That disjoint between case diameter and lug-to-lug is the central trap of buying watches online or by spec sheet alone. If you've been sizing based solely on the "42 mm" or "40 mm" headline number in a listing, you've been working with half the information.

## How do I figure out what lug-to-lug works for *my* wrist?

*This one comes up a lot.* The starting point is measuring yourself -- but not the way most people think.

Measure straight across the top of your wrist, from where it starts to curve down on one side to where it curves down on the other. That flat span is the landing zone for the watch. This is different from wrist circumference, which is what you use for bracelet length.

Once you have that flat-width number, use these working thresholds: around 6.5 inches (165 mm) of circumference, keep lug-to-lug at or under about 47 mm. Around 7 inches (178 mm), 49-50 mm fits comfortably. At 7.5 inches (190 mm) and up, 52 mm and beyond works. These are ceilings. A watch well under the ceiling always looks deliberate; one at the ceiling looks like the biggest thing you could get away with.

A simple rule of thumb: a comfortable lug-to-lug is roughly 75-90% of your wrist's estimated flat width, depending on how snug you like the fit.

---

## Does lug shape change how the lug-to-lug measurement actually *feels* to wear?

Yes -- and it's one of the most underrated factors in watch sizing.

Curved or downward lugs (like those on the Pelagos or Speedmaster) hug the wrist; straight lugs (like the Big Pilot) feel longer. The measured lug-to-lug might be identical on two watches, but the one with strongly downturned lugs will feel significantly more compact because the case follows the wrist's curve rather than projecting past it. If your lug-to-lug is equal to or up to 2 mm longer than your flat wrist width, you're fine if the lugs curve down -- that's why a 50 mm lug-to-lug with curved lugs can wear like a 47-48 mm flat watch.

This is why in-person try-ons, or trusted wrist-shot reviews from people who share your wrist size, are worth seeking out -- especially for borderline cases. A strap change won't rescue lugs that overhang; the case geometry is what it is.

Integrated-bracelet watches add another wrinkle. On a watch like the Tissot PRX, the lug-to-lug number alone doesn't fully capture how tightly the bracelet begins to curve around the wrist -- the first visible links may be part of the case design yet behave differently depending on the person. Check wrist-shot comparisons for these designs.

## Wait -- isn't lug-to-lug the same as lug width? I keep seeing those two terms.

*Readers frequently ask:* these are completely different measurements and it's worth being precise.

| Measurement | What it measures | What it's used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Lug-to-lug** | Case length, tip of upper lugs to tip of lower lugs | Wrist fit -- will it overhang? |
| **Lug width** | Internal gap between the two lugs on one side | Strap/bracelet sizing (e.g. 20 mm, 22 mm) |
| **Case diameter** | Width of the case face | Visual size impression |
| **Case thickness** | Height of the case off the wrist | Cuff clearance, dress-watch stack |

Lug-to-lug is the full top-to-bottom length of the watch, used for fit. Lug width is the small gap between the lugs where the strap attaches (like 20 mm), used for buying straps. Confusing the two is extremely common among newer buyers. Our guide to [watch lug width](/watch-lug-width-explained) covers the strap-sizing side in full; this article is specifically about the fit dimension.

All four numbers matter, but if you're buying online and can only verify one before purchasing, make it lug-to-lug. Diameter tells you how the watch photographs; lug-to-lug tells you how it fits.

## How do I actually find the lug-to-lug spec for a watch I'm considering?

*This one comes up a lot.* Many brand product pages still don't list it -- a frustrating reality in 2026. Here's the practical lookup order:

**1. Check the brand's spec sheet first.** Premium and mid-range brands (Seiko, Tissot, Hamilton, Orient, Christopher Ward) increasingly list it. Brand-listed dimensions, when provided, are accurate to ±0.5 mm; third-party measurements from sources like Hodinkee and Time+Tide are equally reliable.

**2. Trusted review sites.** Watch-specialist outlets routinely caliper test reference watches and publish lug-to-lug alongside other specs. A quick search for "[model name] lug-to-lug" will usually surface a measured number fast.

**3. Community data.** WatchUSeek and Reddit's r/Watches often have owner-measured specs, including wrist shots from people who've noted their wrist circumference. Cross-referencing two or three of these builds a reliable picture.

**4. Use a watch you already own as a reference.** If a watch fits you well, measure its lug-to-lug with a digital caliper or ruler and use that as your personal comfort ceiling. Underdial's wrist-check feature works the same way -- it looks at your existing collection to spot patterns in which specs consistently work for your wrist, so you can apply that to new purchases rather than guessing from scratch every time.

## Some real examples: how much does lug-to-lug vary between popular watches?

Here are measured lug-to-lug figures for watches commonly bought in the under-$1,000 range, so you can see how widely the number varies even at similar diameters:

| Watch | Case Diameter | Lug-to-Lug | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seiko 5 SNK Series | 37 mm | ~46 mm | Compact, suits most wrists |
| Orient Bambino V2 | 40.5 mm | ~48 mm | Mid-length, moderate lugs |
| Tissot PRX (non-integrated) | 40 mm | ~47 mm | Integrated bracelet changes feel |
| Hamilton Khaki Field Auto | 42 mm | ~50 mm | Long lugs, wears larger than diameter suggests |
| Seiko Turtle (SRPE) | 45 mm | ~52 mm | Needs a 170 mm+ wrist to sit cleanly |
| Citizen Promaster Diver | 44 mm | ~53 mm | Built for larger wrists |
| Seiko Alpinist (SARB017) | 38 mm | ~44 mm | Unusually compact for a 38 mm case |

The Seiko Alpinist's ~44 mm lug-to-lug at a 38 mm case diameter is a classic example of why diameter misleads in both directions -- it wears smaller than its case size suggests, which is a big part of why it's beloved on 6.25-6.75-inch wrists.

## I ordered a watch and the lugs definitely overhang. Can anything fix it?

Worth being direct: a strap can't rescue lugs that overhang. The case geometry is what it is. A thinner strap can sometimes soften the visual effect slightly, but it won't change the physical footprint. A NATO or pass-through strap that wraps behind the case can make a long lug-to-lug feel more anchored, but the overhang remains.

Before returning the watch, check whether it actually bothers you in wear. Some buyers find they mind it in photos far more than in daily use -- especially if the lugs curve downward and don't rock the case when it's set face-up on a desk. If the case rocks noticeably when you flex your wrist, or the lugs pinch the underside of your wrist, the lug-to-lug is genuinely too long for your wrist shape.

The honest answer is to prevent this before it happens by checking lug-to-lug before ordering -- especially when buying from brands or retailers without a returns policy, or on grey-market and pre-owned platforms where you may not have a clean return window.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is lug-to-lug on a watch?**
Lug-to-lug (often written L2L) is the distance from the tip of the top lugs to the tip of the bottom lugs, measured parallel to the strap. It tells you how much length the watch case occupies along your wrist -- the dimension most likely to overhang on a smaller wrist.

**What lug-to-lug is right for my wrist?**
A common rule: keep lug-to-lug at or under your wrist's flat width. Around a 6.5-inch (165 mm) wrist circumference, aim for under 47 mm lug-to-lug. Around 7 inches (178 mm), up to 49-50 mm is comfortable. At 7.5 inches (190 mm) or more, 52 mm and beyond can work well.

**Is lug-to-lug the same as lug width?**
No -- completely different measurements. Lug-to-lug is the case length from top lug tip to bottom lug tip, governing wrist fit. Lug width is the internal gap between the two lugs on one side, which tells you what strap size to buy (e.g. 20 mm or 22 mm).

**Can curved lugs make a long lug-to-lug feel shorter?**
Yes, significantly. Downward-curving lugs hug the wrist and reduce the felt footprint compared to straight lugs of the same measured length. A 50 mm lug-to-lug with strongly curved lugs can wear closer to a 47 mm flat-lug design.

**What if a brand doesn't list the lug-to-lug?**
Check a trusted third-party review -- specialist outlets like Hodinkee, Time+Tide, and Worn & Wound routinely measure it. You can also measure a watch you already own that fits well and use that as your personal benchmark. Brands that routinely omit lug-to-lug from spec sheets are worth approaching cautiously.
